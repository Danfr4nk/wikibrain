# The 2019 Ledger

If 2018 was the big year, 2019 is the year he keeps correcting the record on. "Omg you're still in 2018?!?!?" he said the night the receipts were being read, watching 2019 pile up behind it. "There are still 2 more boosted boards and more drones too." Then, watching the sex toys surface: "You're not even to the worst of it yet." Then, when the second Mini X receipt actually appeared on the screen: "That's funny you think I would lie about Boosted Boards. There's nothing I'm going to be more committed to the truth on."

The 2018 entry ended with a miss. His memory of the year ran hotter than the paper trail: only one Boosted board appeared in those receipts, and the entry said so. The 2019 receipts fix it. Both boards are here, dated, priced, and numbered — the correction this entry is required to make (see The predictions audit, and the dated correction appended to the 2018 entry). He was right about the thing he said he'd never lie about.

The dataset: 157 Amazon order-confirmation receipts, read individually from his Gmail, spanning January 1 to December 23, 2019. Total: **$10,981.37**. Unlike 2018 — where the receipts begin mid-July — this year the search caught the whole calendar. No gap. 132 Gmail threads, seven batches, newest to oldest. The complete per-order log — every order number, every truncated item title, every total — is preserved at `raw/self/amazon-2019-orders/amazon-2019-orders.md`.

And the number that matters most is the one he gave while we read: the move. "We moved to New York in February, like early February, February 9th, 2019. Anything purchased from like January 1st up until that date was like getting ready to move to New York." Five weeks of receipts are move-prep, and once you know that, the year's weirdest clusters stop being weird and start being logistics.

## The headline numbers

The receipts were read newest-to-oldest across 132 Gmail threads, in seven batches. The batch log, in full:

| Batch | Window | Orders | Total |
|-------|--------|--------|-------|
| 1 | Dec 23–Aug 4, 2019 | 25 | $959.95 |
| 2 | Aug 4–Jun 1, 2019 | 25 | $979.31 |
| 3 | Jun 1–Mar 26, 2019 | 25 | $1,794.30 |
| 4 | Mar 25–Mar 3, 2019 | 25 | $2,490.57 |
| 5 | Feb 28–Jan 18, 2019 | 25 | $1,776.91 |
| 6 | Jan 18–Jan 9, 2019 | 25 | $2,236.76 |
| 7 | Jan 7–Jan 1, 2019 | 7 | $743.57 |
| **Total** | **Jan 1–Dec 23, 2019** | **157** | **$10,981.37** |

That is roughly $915 a month and an order a little under every other day, sustained across the entire year. It reads as a lighter year than 2018 ($22,931.99 in five and a half months), and in dollars it is — less than half the money, more than twice the calendar. But the distribution is the story. Batches 5 through 7 — January and February, the move window — hold 57 orders and $4,757.24, nearly half the year's total in roughly seven weeks. March (batch 4) is the year's single heaviest month at $2,490.57, driven by the second Boosted Mini X. Then the spending falls off a cliff: the seven months from April through December cost $4,729.56 combined — less than January and February alone.

The shape of the year is therefore not a plateau. It is a launch, a landing, and a long glide. Everything big happens before spring: the move, the boards, the toys, the MacBook. Everything after is the glide — cheaper, thinner, late-year orders at gift-card prices. Read chronologically, 2019 is a year with one beginning and no second act.

One more number before the story: the newest-format Amazon receipt emails, which begin appearing around October 2019, list no item titles at all — just the order number and total. The late-year log is therefore thinner in substance than the early year. The money is complete; the texture fades after autumn.

## The arcs

**The move-prep January.** Dan's framing, given the night the receipts were read: everything from January 1 to February 9 was getting ready to move to New York, and the clusters confirm it. On January 9, at 3:06 in the morning: a Boosted Mini X for $1,076.95. On January 10: the fourth, fifth, and sixth Bern helmets ($96.40, $94.71). On January 11: a 47-piece mechanic's tool set ($113.29), Nike sportswear ($179.39), chalk markers, art supplies. On January 13, between 1:34 and 1:51 AM: vape flavor drops, a BLACK+DECKER drill ($107.19), a bicycle helmet ($42.40), a longboard ($22.89). On January 25: a longboard wall rack ($25.42) — the fleet gets mounted on a wall that didn't exist yet, in a city he hadn't moved to. On January 1: a GoPro HERO7 Black ($443.08), a 128GB SanDisk Extreme ($37.09). On January 9: Wasabi Power batteries ($23.84). The full vlogging rig, bought on day one of the year.

Read as move logistics, the duplicates stop looking compulsive and start looking like planning. Two Mini X's is redundancy — a backup board for a man who commutes on one. Six helmets (Giro Disciple MIPS $149.90 in March, Bern Brentwood $55.52, Bern 2016 Melrose Satin $94.71, Bern Watts EPS $96.40, Bern Unlimited Berkeley $55.61, EVT Safe Zone $42.40) is a helmet for every mode of riding. The drill, the tool set, the wall rack: a man assembling an apartment in advance of arriving in it. The January binge is the most rational spending in either ledger — it just looks insane because it happened at 3 AM in bursts.

**The Boosted fleet, confirmed.** Three line items, and this entry corrects the 2018 record with them. Stealth, $1,695.99, December 2018 (the 2018 Ledger's monument). Mini X, $1,076.95, January 9, 2019, 3:06 AM (#113-3935408-9366663). Mini X, $1,105.65, March 25, 2019, 11:04 PM (#113-5782004-1525831). His prediction from the 2018 read — "there are still 2 more boosted boards" — lands twice, dated and priced, eleven weeks apart. "There's nothing I'm going to be more committed to the truth on," he said when the second one surfaced, and the receipts back him completely. (One honest caveat the ledger can't resolve: the March 25 Mini X could in principle be a rebuy after returning the January 9 unit rather than a second concurrent board. Returns are invisible to this dataset. The two purchases are confirmed; the concurrency is inferred from the supporting ecosystem — belts ×4, the Huck Boys accessory, two rounds of Orangatang Caguama wheels, the wall rack — a man building a fleet, not replacing a unit.)

**The Shelbie chapter.** April 18, 2019: an Apple MacBook Retina for $793.94. It went to Shelbie. His words, the night we read it: "I BOUGHT HER A MACBOOK LOL." And: "I guess it was a refurb." And, of Annie's reaction: she was "fucking PISSED."

The context, as he told it the same night, is the year's most human sequence — and it belongs in this entry because the MacBook only makes sense inside it. They had just moved to New York on February 9. Forty-five days later, back in Pennsylvania for a weekend, he decided on the train to reach out to Shelbie — a stripper who didn't know him, hadn't heard of him — and offer her $700. She showed up. Annie caused a ruckus. He couldn't finish, got mad at Annie, and Annie had to call Shelbie back; a threesome ensued. He fell in love. Two days later he dragged Annie on a ninety-minute-each-way drive to pick Shelbie up from the strip club she was dancing at. He made Annie stay three extra days for another threesome. Back to New York, absorbed by Shelbie. A month later he went back to Pennsylvania alone, carrying what he describes as $4,000 of exotic drugs to seem cool. On his last night he walked out with her car keys in his pocket — her only set. "The end forever lol." He paid to have someone come out that day and cut her a new key; she was, in his words, "still so fucking mad" — "it was completely shot after that."

The ledger's contribution to this story is small and precise: the MacBook, April 18, $793.94, a refurb, bought mid-obsession, landing on a woman he'd known for roughly five weeks. Annie's fury is the only review the purchase ever got. The year's other gift purchases — the 2018 Sephora cards to Ally, the $100 card to his mother — were gestures. This one is a monument to a month.

**The sex-toy era at peak.** The 2018 entry opened the era; 2019 is its busiest year, exactly as he dated it ("it goes until like October 2020 heavy"). The receipts mark two nights as the era's summits.

The first: January 10, 2019, 12:53 to 1:11 AM. Eighteen minutes. A lesbian coloring book ($9.53), "She Comes First: The..." ($16.47), a clitoral-sucking vibrator ($78.42), and a Fleshlight masturbator doll ($84.75). $189.17 in eighteen minutes, after midnight, nine days into the year, in the middle of move-prep. The most rational month in the ledger contains its most concentrated toy run — the two Dans of January, side by side in the timestamps.

The second: February 15–16. On the 15th at 10:58 PM, a Fleshlight Girls | Adriana ($91.10). On the 16th at 5:54 AM and again at 1:39 PM: the same "Chiseled Chad Male Love Doll," $181.31 each, twice in eight hours. The duplicate is the 2018 entry's signature tell — the second purchase happens because the first one didn't close the loop — and here it is at the era's highest dollar line item. The two dolls alone cost more than the entire April-through-December glide's average month.

The era keeps humming all year: June 1, a Jeff Stryker dildo ($60.91); June 3, a spandex hood ($12.19); August 4, a Xise 3D Love Doll ($120.69); August 6, a Passion Lubes Nuru haul ($146.40); October 8, Doc Johnson ($69.83); a wand massager and lubes in the spring batch. It never really stops — it just gets cheaper and more routine, the way obsessions do when the first hundred orders have already been placed.

And then the postscript, his words: "I returned a lot of sex toys too. Dont ever buy sex toys ob Amazon or you may get my one time used and returned sex toys too." This is the one line in the whole two-year project that the receipts cannot check — returns are invisible to order confirmations — and it's also the one that most changes how the totals read. The $10,981.37 is gross, and he is telling you, laughing, that some of it came back. The era's real cost is somewhere under the number, by an amount nobody can reconstruct. The line is kept here anyway, in his own spelling, because the warning is the point and the honesty is the data.

**The creator setup.** Early January builds the rig: GoPro HERO7 Black ($443.08), SanDisk Extreme 128GB ($37.09), Wasabi Power batteries ($23.84), an action video adapter kit ($21.15). Late summer builds the studio: a ring light and a Logitech webcam (batch 1, August–December window) — the classic creator two-shot. The year's bookends are a man assembling the ability to be seen, first on a board in the street, then at a desk. Between them: "The Hidden Tools of Comedy" ($69.73, January 12, 4:17 AM) and the Comedy Bang! Bang! seasons 1, 3, and 4 (March) — the comedy arc that had been running since the 2018 book sprees keeps its receipts.

**The comedy-and-books arc.** 2018 bought frameworks in bulk at dawn; 2019 buys the performance. The Hidden Tools of Comedy. Comedy Bang! Bang!. Onnit and comedy books in the summer batch. Onnit Shroom Tech, twice, and a testosterone booster in the spring — the men's-health cluster, sitting right next to the wand massager in the same window, the two shopping carts of a man optimizing every system he can reach.

**The helmet collection.** Six helmets in one year, four of them in January, all of it move-prep on paper and all of it absurd in practice. The Giro Disciple MIPS ($149.90) is the serious one — full-face downhill certification, bought in March, after the move. The rest are the collection: Bern Brentwood ($55.52), Bern 2016 Melrose Satin ($94.71), Bern Watts EPS ($96.40), Bern Unlimited Berkeley ($55.61), EVT Safe Zone Bicycle ($42.40). Nobody needs six helmets. The 2018 entry's line about the 29 pairs of socks applies with one word changed: nobody decides their helmet situation is a crisis at 1 AM on a plan.

**The cat cluster.** The pet era continues, and it has a name now: Gabe's gear. Two PetSafe ScoopFree self-cleaning litter boxes (March, batch 4) plus a litter tray ($49.77, January 18). MEOWFIA cat furniture ($58.29). A KONG Scrunch Knots squirrel ($25.18). Two Meowijuana Purrple Passion catnip pouches ($21.18, January 16). The 2018 entry had Milo's costume at 2:35 AM; 2019 is the cat's year, and the spending is calmer, steadier, daytime — the one category in the ledger that reads like maintenance instead of compulsion.

**The gift-card economy, continued.** 2018's forty-one $0.00 orders set the pattern; 2019 keeps it running through the fall batch, where gift-card-covered orders appear again. The mechanism is unchanged: balance stops feeling like money, and the late-year orders — cheaper, thinner, after the big spending ended — ride on it. The year's glide is partly a gift-card glide.

**The outlier orders.** An iRobot Braava jet ($199) — the robot mop, joining 2018's robot vacuum lineage, the home automating itself one appliance at a time. Big Lebowski, Fight Club, and Abducted in Plain Sight rentals. The Life Aquatic for $4.23 (his Wes Anderson canon, priced like a rounding error). An Apple 85W MagSafe 2 power adapter ($106.33) — keeping the old machine alive. A Black Diamond climbing accessory ($8.43) — the one item in the ledger that suggests an entire sport that never appears again. Capella Flavor Drops ($6.40) — the vape phase, one line item, gone.

**The candy note.** His claim, the night we read: "I bought a lot of candy too." The receipts as captured don't break it out — the truncated item titles don't surface a candy category the way 2018's six pounds of Runts (twice) did. The claim is kept here as his, unconfirmed by the paper trail, which is itself a small illustration of the ledger's asymmetry: the corpus confirms his categories better than his quantities, and his memory adds categories the corpus truncates.

## The impulsivity is in the metadata

The 2018 entry's thesis — daylight Dan buys golf clubs, night Dan buys everything else — survives the second year with a complication. 2019 has the same circadian rhythm (January 10's 18-minute run at 1 AM; the January 9 Mini X at 3:06 AM; comedy books at 4:17 AM), but the move-prep window scrambles the moral of it. The January clusters are the year's most compulsive-looking buying and also its most functional: the drill, the tool set, the helmets, the wall rack are the right purchases, bought at the wrong hours. Impulsivity and planning turn out to share a timestamp.

The new tell, and the one 2019 adds to the taxonomy, is the **same-day duplicate**: the twin male dolls on February 16, eight hours apart, $362.62 total. The 2018 duplicates were weeks apart (the wedge) or minutes apart at midnight (the watches). The dolls are something else — a purchase repeated after a full morning in between, which means the loop didn't close even after the first one arrived. That is the dataset's clearest single illustration of the year's real subject, and it isn't impulsivity. It's the thing impulsivity is failing to fix.

The clusters persist as the unit of purchase. January 11: four orders between 1:13 and 6:33 PM (art supplies, chalk markers, tools, sportswear). January 13, 1:34–1:51 AM: four orders in seventeen minutes. February 16: two dolls, eight hours. March's Boosted belt run (four belts, $69.18) arrives with the Giro helmet and the Braava — the support infrastructure for the fleet, bought as a system. Something gets decided — usually at night, sometimes across a whole day — and then it gets executed completely, immediately, across as many carts as it takes. The cluster is still the unit. The year just gave it better reasons.

## The predictions audit

The 2018 entry closed with an audit of the three predictions Dan made the night the receipts were read. Two landed; one missed. This entry settles the miss.

**Two more Boosted boards.** Confirmed — twice. January 9 ($1,076.95) and March 25 ($1,105.65), both Mini X, both in these receipts, eleven weeks apart. The 2018 entry's miss is corrected in full (see the dated correction appended to that entry). His memory of the category was exact; the paper trail just needed the next year to catch up. "There's nothing I'm going to be more committed to the truth on" — and the receipts agree with him.

**More sex toys, heavy through October 2020.** Confirmed, and then some. 2019 is the era's busiest year in these receipts — the January 10 run, the February 16 twins, the summer doll-and-Nuru sequence, the October Doc Johnson order. The category prediction from 2018 was perfect; the 2019 receipts are the evidence for it.

**More drones.** Absent. No new drone platforms appear in the 2019 receipts — the Mavic arc that filled in exactly as predicted in 2018 does not extend. The 2018 drones were the arc; 2019's money went to boards and wheels instead. One of three predictions misses in the second year, and it's the one about the category he was already done with.

The deeper finding from the two-year audit is the one the 2018 entry named: his predictions about the *categories* are perfect and his quantities run hot — except on the thing he cares about most, where the quantity was exact and the paper trail was just a year behind. He knows the shape of his own compulsions. On Boosted boards, he knows the count too.

## The hidden economy

The $450-a-day figure from the 2018 entry belongs here as well — his words, "through this whole era," and the era in these receipts runs the full year. On the order of $164,000 a year underneath a $10,981 ledger, while unemployed, in a year that also included a cross-state move and a $793.94 refurbished MacBook for a woman he'd known five weeks. The receipts are the visible fraction of an economy he named himself, laughing, in the same conversation both years. The stash safes and spy bolts were 2018's logistics; 2019's logistics are the drill, the tool set, and the wall rack — the habit's hardware upgraded into the move's hardware, and the ledger can't tell the difference.

## Limits

What this dataset is not. The search covered ihatedanfrank only, for the full calendar year — and the newest receipt is December 23, so the Christmas week tail is thin or absent. Dan has said dfrank88 carried purchase history for a long stretch, which means this ledger is one mailbox's view of the year, not necessarily the whole of it. The receipts are order confirmations only; returns, cancellations, and refunds are invisible — and he has stated, on the record, that he returned a lot of sex toys, so the $10,981.37 is gross by an unknowable margin. Item titles are truncated as captured from the receipt emails; from roughly October 2019 the newer email format lists no item titles at all, so the autumn log is money without texture. The two Mini X orders are confirmed purchases; whether they represent two concurrent boards or a return-and-rebuy is inferred, not proven. The $450-a-day figure is his stated number, not a measured one.

## Method

Amazon's own order history was unreachable (bot-detection block, September 2026), so the receipts were read live from his Gmail: search `from:amazon.com after:2019-01-01 before:2020-01-01 "order confirmation"`, 132 threads, each receipt opened individually, newest to oldest, with date, order number, item titles, and total extracted per order. Seven batches, 157 orders. The complete per-order log — every order number, every truncated item title, every total — is preserved at `raw/self/amazon-2019-orders/amazon-2019-orders.md`.

The $164,000 is the context. The timestamps are still the diagnosis.*
