# Seth LeDonne

Seth LeDonne is a Uniontown-scene friend who occupies a small thread
and a large debt. The thread is a single Facebook conversation from the
spring of 2020 — 47 messages across three and a half
months — and it carries two things at once: a real-time account of New
York City in the lockdown-and-protest season, and one of the rare
moments in Dan's corpus where he names, specifically and by name, the
people he credits for his taste.

The debt is the larger half. On April 17, 2020, unprompted, Dan wrote
Seth an extended thank-you for an adolescent education in hardcore and
punk counterculture — naming Seth alongside Andy, Paul, Nathan, and
Kessler as the mentors whose disdain taught him what authenticity was
supposed to cost. It is a strange, warm, deliberately masochistic
letter: gratitude for cruelty, offered two decades later, by a man who
almost never attributes his own formation to anyone.

The two halves of the thread belong together. The man who wrote the
curfew dispatches from Manhattan in June 2020 — watching the George
Floyd-protest curfew land on his neighborhood and describing it as
"like living in beruit" [sic] — is legible partly through the teenager
the scene made: suspicious of whatever the room is applauding, fluent
in disdain, and still, in April 2020, unwilling to give his vote to
the corporate Democratic machinery that had, in his words, "spoiled
the nomination process." The thread is short. What it documents is
not.

## The scene and its mentors

Dan came up through the Uniontown-area hardcore and punk scene of the
early 2000s, and by his own account the scene was not welcoming — it
was a standard he failed against, repeatedly, in public. The fixed
points he names are people, not bands: Seth, Andy, Paul, Nathan,
Kessler. Around them orbits a zine he names in the same breath —
"rejected gazette," in his own lowercase — which he names as a
touchstone of the same milieu,
and the basement-show circuit where his high school band,
**Batteries Not
Included**, operated inside the mentors' jurisdiction rather than
beside it.

The texture of that jurisdiction, as Dan reconstructs it decades
later, is enforcement through contempt. He recalls being mocked for
wearing a "311 concert" tie-dye shirt to a hardcore basement show —
the kind of error the scene existed to punish — and he remembers the
punishment as the point. The scene's "visceral disdain" for anything
perceived as inauthentic was, in his retrospective phrase, "really
developmental for me later in life."

That retrospective credit matters because of how rarely Dan gives it.
Across the corpus, his tastes — the electronic production, the
shock-radio archive, the political instincts — mostly present as
self-generated, the output of a man who found things on his own and
corrected himself on his own. The mentors are one of the few places
the record shows him handing the credit to specific people and meaning
it. The wiki's own cross-references make the same point from the other
side: **Opie and Anthony** is
documented as the other great teenage counterculture immersion, the
shock-radio archive absorbed through years of solo listening, and the
two are explicitly paired — both "formative teenage counterculture
immersions Dan explicitly credits decades later," training "the same
anti-normie, no-flinch aesthetic register." One immersion was social
and enforced by people who could mock him to his face; the other was
solitary and enforced by nobody. The register came out the same.

The scene was not a phase he passed through and discarded, either.
Batteries Not Included played hardcore and punk covers alongside
originals Dan wrote as the band's lead songwriter, inside the same
standards the mentors enforced — which is to say the debt was not
abstract. The scene gave him a band to be in, a room to be judged in,
and a definition of fraud he has spent the rest of the record trying
not to be.

That last point deserves its full size, because it is the mechanism
the letter is really about. A scene that works the way Dan describes
this one — disdain as quality control, authenticity as the only
currency, fraud as the unforgivable sin — does not produce alumni so
much as it produces internalized enforcement. The kid mocked for the
tie-dye shirt does not need the mentors in the room anymore; he has
installed them. Everything later in Dan's record that reads as
temperament — the contempt for managed consensus, the refusal to
perform enthusiasm for a process he considers rigged, the instinct
that applause is evidence against — has, on the evidence of this one
letter, an earlier address: a basement show, a wrong shirt, and five
people who would not pretend otherwise. The April 17 letter is Dan
acknowledging the installation, twenty years on, to the man whose
name the wiki files it under. That is why a mentor gets a page. The
others in the group are named in it; the thread that carried the
acknowledgment was Seth's.

## The April 17 letter

The tribute arrives on April 17, 2020, unprompted — no occasion, no
news hook, nothing in the thread's rhythm that requires it. Dan is
thirty-one, living through the first lockdown spring in New York, and
he writes back across roughly two decades to assign credit. It came
in three messages over twelve minutes; the first carries the debt
itself:

> "just another overdue moment of deserved praise... growing up in
> 'the scene' and being exposed to things like rejected gazette and
> the entire vibe of things..... it really had such a lasting impact
> on me and I'm certain that i wouldn't have the appreciation i do
> for counterculture if not for you, andy, paul, Nathan, kessler"

The letter's argument is that the cruelty was the curriculum. He
frames the group's harshness as productive rather than damaging —
"the visceral disdain that I felt from the entire group was really
developmental for me later in life" — recalling the mocked 311
tie-dye shirt at the basement show not as a wound but as tuition. And
then he closes with the line the whole letter exists to deliver:

> "tl;dr - thank you for being so cruel about what an enormous fraud
> and p0seur i was because it was the catalyst for finding some
> authenticity"

Read carefully, the letter is doing something more precise than
nostalgia. It is a theory of taste stated as gratitude: authenticity
is not discovered, it is *enforced* — beaten into a kid by older kids
whose contempt he had to survive — and the enforcement only works if
the standard is real. The mentors were not performing cruelty for its
own sake; they were, in Dan's construction, quality control with no
patience for him, and the letter thanks them for not having any.

It also completes a circuit the corpus otherwise leaves open. Dan's
adult persona — the forensic skepticism, the refusal of consensus,
the instinct that the respectable reading is the rigged one — is
usually narrated by the record as temperament, something he simply
is. The April 17 letter is the one place he dates it, places it, and
hands it to five named people in a Uniontown scene. Whatever else the
thread is, it is the closest thing the corpus holds to an origin story
he wrote himself.

## What the letter is evidence of

The letter's evidentiary value is in its anomalies, and they are
worth enumerating because they are what separates this thread from
the thousands of friendly exchanges elsewhere in the corpus.

Dan almost never does what this letter does. His formative
influences, where the record touches them at all, are usually things
— broadcasts, records, archives — consumed alone and reconstructed
after the fact. The Opie and Anthony parallel is the clean contrast:
years of solo listening, no one present, no one to thank, the credit
assigned to an archive. The April 17 letter is the opposite shape.
The influence is a *group*, the mechanism is *enforcement in person*,
and the credit is assigned to five living people by name, in writing,
in a message they could answer. Nothing else in the Seth record —
and very little anywhere in Dan's — works that way.

The timing is also part of the evidence. The letter is written six
weeks into a lockdown, from inside a city emptying itself, by a man
whose present had just been radically thinned. The corpus cannot
say whether the inventory impulse — writing back twenty years to
settle a taste-debt — was caused by the moment; that would be an
inference past the record. What the record shows is sequence: March
brings the shocked dispatches ("people don't really get how
drastically different it is to exist here"), April 14 brings the
political refusal, and April 17 brings the letter to the mentors.
The formation gets inventoried in the same weeks the present is
being dismantled. Whatever produced that ordering, the thread is
where it happened.

And the debt is stated in a form that cannot be collected on. Seth
and the mentors have no lever on a thirty-one-year-old in Manhattan
in 2020; the letter asks for nothing, proposes nothing, and is not
repaid. That asymmetry is consistent with the one quality Dan
repeatedly credits the scene itself with: a standard maintained for
its own sake. He thanks them in their own currency — a judgment,
delivered flatly, with no flattery in it.

## The COVID spring, in real time

The thread opens on March 10, 2020, and its first register is the
shock of early-lockdown New York. Dan is in the city as the pandemic
arrives, and the March exchange has him describing a life already
reorganized by it — "people don't really get how drastically
different it is to exist here" — the particular isolation of watching
a catastrophe from inside its first American epicenter while the rest
of the country still treated it as weather elsewhere.

By April 14 the conversation turns political, and it turns in a way
that dates precisely. Dan still votes in Pennsylvania, and he tells
Seth he feels "extra responsible about it" for exactly that reason —
and then, more than six months before the general election, refuses
the vote the responsibility is supposed to produce:

> "i still vote in PA and subsequently feel like, extra responsible
> about it...but i do not think i can vote biden in november... i feel
> so gross giving my vote to the corporate DNC forces that spoiled
> the nomination process"

The position is an unresolved holdout, and it stays unresolved in the
thread: the objection is not to Biden's electability but to the
process that produced him — the "corporate DNC forces" line makes it
an institutional refusal, not a candidate preference. It is also, in
miniature, the same instinct the mentors get credited for: the
consensus product, arrived at by a managed process, is the fraud; the
holdout is the authenticity. The thread does not record how, or
whether, he finally voted that November.

Two details in the April 14 exchange reward slowing down. The first
is the anchor: "i still vote in PA." Dan is living in Manhattan,
describing Manhattan's curfew and Manhattan's lockdown, and his
civic registration still sits in Pennsylvania — Uniontown's orbit —
with the felt responsibility running back along it ("extra
responsible about it"). The record does not explain the arrangement,
but it fixes the shape of his 2020: physically in the city coming
apart, bureaucratically still a Pennsylvanian, and feeling the
second fact as a duty precisely because of the distance. The second
detail is the date. This is the earliest dated instance the corpus
holds of the refusal pattern that recurs across his later political
writing — the objection aimed at the *process* ("spoiled the
nomination process") rather than the candidate, with the holdout
posture presented as the honest one. In 2020 it is a private message
to an old scene friend. The pattern's later public life is
documented elsewhere in the wiki; its first datable appearance is
here.

The thread's sharpest moment comes on June 7, 2020. New York is under
an 8pm curfew in the George Floyd-protest period, and Dan reports
from inside it in real time, in three short messages:

> "8pm curfew"
> "it's been like living in beruit" [sic]
> "that's pretty much what all of manhattan has been like until today"

The comparison is doing reportorial work, not rhetorical:
a curfew, an emptied and militarized street grid, a city whose normal
signals had been replaced by emergency ones. Seth asks directly
whether Dan attended protests — and then, thirteen seconds later,
withdraws his own question: "Actually let's talk about this later
I WILL CALL," citing a class he had to finish by twelve. The question
was never Dan's to deflect; it was Seth's to postpone, and Dan's only
reply was "go! talk later." Two weeks of diminishing contact follow,
and the thread ends on June 23 with a missed-call notice: Seth
called, Dan missed it. The zine Seth had offered to mail went, on
this record, unconfirmed.

What the thread amounts to, taken whole, is a three-act contemporaneous
record: the pandemic arriving (March), the politics souring (April),
the protests and the curfew (June) — narrated by one participant to
one friend, in something close to real time, with the counterculture
letter sitting in the middle of it like a thesis statement. The
corpus record is small:

## The questions the thread leaves open

The thread ends the way it documents: abruptly, and with its most
direct question answered by nobody. Seth asks whether Dan attended
the protests — the only question in the exchange aimed at what Dan
*did* rather than what he saw or believed — and then withdraws it
himself, thirteen seconds later, before Dan can answer: "Actually
let's talk about this later I WILL CALL." Dan's attendance at the
2020 protests is therefore genuinely open, but for a reason worth
stating precisely: the question was postponed by its asker, not
evaded by its subject. It was never answered because it was never
re-asked in any held record. Whatever Dan did or didn't do that
June, this thread does not say, and closing the question here would
be invention.

The other loose ends are smaller and stay smaller. Seth called on
June 23; the record carries only the missed-call notice, so whether
they ever spoke is unrecorded. A zine mailing was offered — Seth's
own zine, address exchanged — and its arrival never confirmed.
Seth's circumstances after June 2020 are not in the held corpus at
all. These are the ordinary gaps of a correspondence that stops
rather than concludes — noted here at their true size, which is
small, and at their true status, which is unresolved.

## Assessment

Seth LeDonne's entry is small because the record is small, and its
weight is out of proportion to its size for two reasons. First, the
April 17 letter is the corpus's clearest instance of Dan naming the
human sources of his taste — five people, one scene, one standard —
in a body of work that otherwise narrates its own formation as
solitary. Second, the June 7 curfew account is contemporaneous,
timestamped witness evidence of New York's lockdown-and-protest
spring, held in Dan's own words and now verified against them.

The friendship itself the record barely keeps: one thread, one
spring, a missed call at the end of it. What survives is the
debt, stated once and plainly, by the man who owed it.

What would still change this entry is not verification — the quotes
are verified now — but continuation: anything of Seth past June
2020, or of Dan at the 2020 protests, sits outside the held record.
Until evidence for either surfaces, the entry stands as corrected.

## Cliff notes

- **Batteries Not Included (BNI)** — Batteries Not Included — "BNI" in Dan's own shorthand — was his high school-era band with Joe Oshnack and a third member, Matt Turko, playing hardcore/punk covers alongside originals Dan wrote as, in his own words, "lead songwriter." It predates every other music project the wiki documents by nearly a decade — the earliest evidence of the same instinct that later becomes SLOPPP, MOGZART, and GRIPNOTIC.
- **Opie and Anthony** — The single most-watched thing in Dan's entire YouTube history is not music, politics, or a game — it is the Opie & Anthony shock-radio archive. The watch-history page (Youtube Watch History) records it as raw channel counts, but those counts describe a formative obsession that shaped his sense of humor, his idiom, and the comedy taste documented on Stand Up Comedy. This page is the "lived meaning" behind the numbers.
- **COVID era 2020** — The pandemic year lands in the middle of the first NYC chapter's later stretch and directly overlaps the documented 2020–2021 market era. It is also the year the Fran estate finally pays — five advances between February and June, a court order on August 21, and a six-figure deposit on September 1 — and the year the record holds Dan's own narration of a political conversion, timestamped to the minute.
