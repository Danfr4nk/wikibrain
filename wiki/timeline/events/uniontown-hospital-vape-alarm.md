# The Uniontown Hospital Vape Alarm (early April 2018)

During the four-day death watch at Uniontown Hospital, in the pediatric wing
where [[wiki/people/fran-coldren|Fran Coldren]] was dying of a deliberate
water shutdown, Dan Frank stepped into the room's bathroom, took one pull
from a high-wattage sub-ohm vaporizer, set off the smoke detector, and
triggered a fire alarm that chain-spread across the entire hospital. Four
fire trucks arrived within minutes. Firefighters and a security guard
entered the room; Dan told them the device "went off in my pocket"; they
shook their heads, killed the alarms, and walked him off the property, where
the guard wrote up charging documents.

Those documents were real: a summary non-traffic case was filed with the
Fayette County magistrate on **April 5, 2018**, the day after Fran died, under
docket **MJ-14101-NT-0000082-2018**, on a single charge of *smoking* in a
hospital. It is the corpus's second documented brush with charges, and by
some distance its most disproportionate: the thing obtained was one drag of
nicotine.

The page exists for three reasons, and they are all downstream of that one
drag. First, the reasoning Dan says he ran before the act — an engineering
argument, performed first and found sound, about combustion particulates and
detectors — is the corpus's purest specimen of top-percentile intellect
recruited to authorize an impulse rather than interrogate it, and the 2026
retelling quietly revises whether the reasoning did the authorizing at all.
Second, the Commonwealth's answer declined his distinction entirely: it
charged him with *smoking*. Third, the court paper accidentally documented
where he lived in a month the wiki had been reconstructing from testimony
alone — a dated official record, filed seven days after an eviction notice,
placing him at his mother's house.

## The four weeks around it

The alarm does not belong to an ordinary hospital visit. It belongs to the
last four weeks of Fran Coldren's life, and the sequence matters because
every pressure in it is already at maximum when the detector trips.

The night of March 7–8, 2018, Fran fell — "Down Goes Frazier," the second
fall, the one the corpus can date. Dan and Annie took the overnight, and the
next morning's messages document the ambulance: Dan to Vicki, *"she was
screaming so loud when they were putting her on the gurney it was just
awful"* — verified in the held iMessage corpus, 2018-03-08 18:29:31 UTC
(`kb/data/0169-fran-vigil-fall-and-hospital-sequence.md`). From the hospital
she was mandated to a nursing home — the outcome her stay-at-home directive
was built to prevent — and within about two weeks her state failed
completely.

On March 29, mid-collapse, Dan was served an eviction notice terminating his
residency at 155 Virginia Ave and demanding the keys — served by "Dian V.
Moore," identified 2026-08-18 as [[wiki/people/diane-moore|Diane Moore]]
herself, Fran's only child and the estate's institutional pole. The estate
was moving on the property before Fran was gone.

On **April 1, 2018** — the first day of caddie season — Fran was moved to
the Uniontown Hospital pediatric wing to die. Judge Fred Adams, Ira's old
law partner and trustee of the estate accounts, mandated a 24-hour rotation,
held in shifts by Dan, Annie, Vicki, and Marla. Dan had been paid $15/hour
for the preceding six months to care for Fran full time — his contemporaneous
account as quoted on the vigil record, with no payroll record checked, but
the figure that names what the vigil was: not a visit, but the end of a job.
Diane's letter had already excluded him from the shift rotation; the
exclusion and the vigil overlapped without either one being the operative
constraint.

Then the water was stopped — the deliberate shutdown, Fran's own directive
— and the four-day death watch began. Fran died **April 4, 2018**, with Dan
and Annie the two in the room at the moment itself. His contemporaneous
verdict, in his own words: *"fran was happy when she died SPECIFICALLY
because annie and i were there with her."* Three minutes later it began to
snow outside Uniontown Hospital. The alarm happened inside this window —
between the April 1 admission and the April 4 death, on the capture's "day
four of the water shutdown," which points at April 3 or 4. One day, give or
take, before or on the day she died.

That is the room the alarm went off in. Everything else on this page should
be read against it.

## What happened

Per Dan's account, he stepped into the room's bathroom and took one pull
from a high-wattage sub-ohm vaporizer — a SMOK box mod, 110 watts in the
docket-era telling, 220 watts in his 2026 retelling. The smoke detector
tripped, and the alarm chain-spread across the entire hospital. Four fire
trucks arrived within minutes (six, in the 2026 telling — the conflict is
filed low; the alias "four firetrucks" keeps the earlier figure).

Firefighters entered the room — in the 2026 telling, with axes — and a
security guard with them. Dan told them the device "went off in my pocket."
They shook their heads, killed the alarms, and walked him off the property,
where the guard wrote up charging documents. Per his account, he sat down
on the couch next to [[wiki/people/annie-ulmer|Annie]], who had no idea what
had happened, and said nothing at all until the firefighters walked in. (The
2026 telling revises this beat: it has him open the bathroom door, find
Annie ignorant of the cause, and ask her "what's that noise about?" — also
filed low.)

The 2026-10-04 retelling, his fullest to date, adds texture the earlier
accounts lack. Before the pull, he says, he inspected the detector —
*"No lasers. Nothing weird or fancy or a return catch for a laser"* — and
concluded optical detection was not present. Then, in his own words, he
*"ripped a 220W Smok box mod 'out of habit.'"* The detector, per the same
telling, had an optical/laser system after all: the inspection missed what
it was looking for. The telling adds two details nothing earlier had: the
truck that took his car had a lift, and his car was damp from the rain. The
record does not say why the car needed a truck; the detail arrives without
explanation, which is worth noting rather than smoothing over.

The cover line, however, is stable across every telling, from the docket
era to 2026: "It was in my pocket and it accidentally fired which made the
vapor come out." The firefighters' response to it — the head-shake — is the
only contemporary review of the story the record holds.

His account of the aftermath is timed to the second and worth keeping in
his proportions: five seconds to understand what had happened, one second
to grasp the magnitude — the coming embarrassment, the "bone shaking
stupidity of the act itself," and the absurdity of setting off a mass
emergency response while his great-grandmother, so dehydrated she looked
like a skeleton, lay dying in the middle of the room. Four days without
food or water, in the 2026 telling's phrasing. Feet away.

The hospital's own record — an incident report, a trespass notice — is
still unsought and is now the only thing that would establish whether Dan
was permitted back onto the property before Fran died. That matters because
Diane's letter had already excluded him from the shift rotation; the two
exclusions may have overlapped without either being the operative one. And
Fran died the next day, or the day of, with him in the room — so whatever
the hospital's paperwork said, the family rotation, or the moment itself,
put him back at the bedside.

## The reasoning, which is the actual content

What distinguishes this from an ordinary lapse is that it was not one —
or so the docket-era account insists. Dan's account is emphatic that he ran
the argument first and found it sound: smoke detectors respond to
*combustion* particulates; a vaporizer does not burn anything; the two
aerosols look alike but are physically different; the detector, correctly
understood, would agree with him. He describes himself in that moment by
the subculture's own title — "it's 2018, I'm a fucking cloud king" — and
notes that cloud kings are not stupid. The deduction is valid in form and
false in its premise, which is precisely the shape
[[wiki/mind/profile/big-five-psychometrics]] describes as the
intellect/impulsivity paradox: top-percentile reasoning recruited *to
authorize* an impulse rather than to interrogate it. The reasoning did not
slow the urge down. It cleared it for takeoff.

That was the page's thesis for years. The 2026 retelling revises it, and
the revision is the most interesting thing the retelling contains. *"Ripped
a 220W Smok box mod 'out of habit'"* is not the language of a deduction
clearing an urge for takeoff; it is the language of the urge firing first
and the deduction being the story told about it afterward. The telling
still has him performing the argument — the detector inspection is new, and
more elaborate than anything in the earlier accounts — but the inspection
*failed*: he looked for optical detection, concluded it was absent, and the
detector tripped anyway. So the 2026 version has him reasoning harder,
checking empirically, getting the check wrong, and acting from habit
regardless.

Both versions cannot be the causal story. In the docket-era version, the
reasoning authorizes the act. In the 2026 version, the reasoning
accompanies the act and the habit does the work. The page keeps both
because both are his, eight years apart, and the drift between them is
itself evidence — of how the story of a stupid act gets re-engineered each
time it is told, with the intellect given a larger or smaller role
depending on the telling. What is stable is the one line that never
changes: the deduction was valid in form and false in its premise, and the
detector did not care.

There is a second, quieter revision inside the retelling. The docket-era
account has the argument as something he *thought through* — a chain of
propositions. The 2026 telling has him *inspecting hardware* — looking at
the detector, judging its sensor type by eye. The argument moved from
physics to forensics, and the forensics were wrong. The cloud king looked
at the detector and declared it safe, and the detector disagreed.

## The priming

Dan attributes the act, hedged, to something he had watched a few hours
earlier: Suz standing in the hospital room taking small drags from a tiny
eJoy vape, "just not even spoken of, it happens so naturally." His own
gloss — "this action must have absorbed into my subconscious somehow" — is
the only causal account anyone has offered, and it is worth taking
seriously in the specific form he gives it. What transferred was not the
idea of vaping; it was the observation that nobody had objected. The
family's characteristic move of normalizing the unremarkable-in-context is,
here, the thing that produced a consequence.

Note the asymmetry the priming account quietly admits: Suz's small pen at
the bedside produced no incident — small puff, no detector trip — while his
sub-ohm cloud in the bathroom produced four fire trucks. The priming
explains the *permission*; it does not explain the *scale*. The scale came
from the device and the room he chose. The 2026 telling puts Suz's pen at
30 watts against the earlier 10 — filed low with the other figure drift —
but the point stands at either wattage: hers was the act that stayed
inside the room's tolerance, his was the one that exceeded it by an order
of magnitude.

## The legal residue — a filed summary case, docket MJ-14101-NT-0000082-2018

| field | value |
|---|---|
| Docket | MJ-14101-NT-0000082-2018 |
| Citation | R 2009305-4 |
| Case filed | 2018-04-05 |
| Summons printed | 2018-04-05, 2:51:15 PM |
| Court | MDJ-14-1-01, Hon. Michael M. Metros, 88 N. Gallatin Ave, Uniontown PA |
| Defendant address of record | 337 Saratoga Dr, Uniontown PA 15401 |
| Charge (sole, lead) | LO § 1639 §§ b 1 — "Smoking prohibited w/in certain public premises including hospital properties" |
| Fine and costs | printed on the sheet, not legible in the surviving frames |
| Plea stub | undetached, both signature lines blank |
| Document photographed | 2018-04-17, 4:31 PM and 4:41 PM, Uniontown |

**The charge is smoking.** One count, marked `(Lead)` with nothing under it
— a local ordinance violation, the smallest instrument the Commonwealth had
available. Four fire trucks turned out, an alarm chain-spread through an
entire hospital, and no one filed for causing a false alarm (18 Pa.C.S.
§ 4905), disorderly conduct, criminal mischief or trespass. This cuts
against the disproportion reading the page has carried, and it is worth
being precise about which direction: the paperwork is grotesquely out of
proportion to *one drag of nicotine*, and simultaneously far *under*
proportion to the emergency response it caused. Fayette County priced the
incident as a smoking violation. That is a narrower answer than either
"nothing happened" or "he was charged for the alarm," and it is the one on
the docket.

There is a second thing the charge does, which the reasoning section above
sets up without knowing it. Dan's entire argument was that **a vaporizer
does not burn anything** — no combustion, no smoke, therefore no detector
and no offence. The charge the Commonwealth filed is *smoking*, at a
hospital, under an ordinance written for cigarettes. The citation does not
engage his distinction; it declines it. The one document in the record
that responds to the cloud king's deduction responds by categorising the
act as the thing he had proved it was not.

The reading of the charge as a Uniontown local ordinance rests on `LO`
being the MDJS charge-type prefix for one — convention, not anything
printed on the sheet. The ordinance's text — what conduct no. 1639 (b)(1)
actually reaches, and whether it names vaporizers or only combustion — is
outside the corpus. It is the document that would say whether Dan's
combustion argument had any legal purchase at all, and nobody has pulled
it.

**The response is not recorded — on the paper.** Both plea lines are blank
and the stub is undetached in a photograph taken twelve days after the
summons was printed — which is past the ten-day window the form specifies,
though the receipt date rather than the print date starts that clock and
the receipt date is unknown. But the docket's last open cell — what the
case cost him — was finally answered, by testimony, on 2026-10-04: the
operator, asked for the first time, said he appeared, performed contrition
for the judge ("I know what a serious situation a fire in a hospital is"),
and the citation cost him about $125, an amount he credits to the
performance. The figure is testimony, not docket-verified — the
Pennsylvania UJS web portal the summons itself names would settle it in
one lookup, from outside the corpus, and nobody has run it — but it is
the first answer the record has ever had to the question the page carried
as its live gap for years. Nothing in eleven years of message record
mentions a court date, a fine, or this docket; the operator's memory,
finally asked, is the only source that ever spoke.

**What the timing shows.** The case was filed **April 5, 2018** — the day
after Fran died on April 4. The summons was printed at 2:51 PM that
afternoon and addressed to 337 Saratoga Drive. It would have arrived in the
mail during the week of her funeral. Nothing in any telling of this story
mentions the envelope; the two videos of the sheet, shot ten minutes apart
on **April 17**, are the only trace that it landed at all, and the fact
that he filmed it twice is the closest thing to a reaction the record
holds. The video the frames come from is not on disk — only three
screenshots of it playing in Photos. Recovering the two clips would likely
resolve the fine-and-costs row and the barcode block, and the 4:31 clip
may pan across fields these frames cut off, the offence date among them.

Alongside the 2015 retail theft ([[wiki/legal/2015-retail-theft-arrest]])
it is the second time a want of essentially zero value ran all the way to
official paperwork — and now the second time that paperwork has a docket
number attached to it. Both were filed in the same magisterial district,
before the same judge, three years apart:
[[wiki/legal/2015-possession-arrest]] records Michael Metros from the
February 2015 court blotter and states that he "appears once ... and has
no other trace in the corpus." He appears twice. MDJ-14-1-01 is the
constant in Dan's charging record; the offences have nothing else in
common.

## The address of record

The summons gives the defendant's address as **337 Saratoga Dr, Uniontown
PA 15401**, printed April 5, 2018 — and that is a fact about where Dan
lived, filed by a court, in a period the wiki has been reconstructing
entirely from testimony and message metadata. [[wiki/places/155-virginia-ave|155
Virginia Avenue]] is where the residence timeline puts him from January
2015 to February 2019, and that page carries an eviction notice served on
him at 155 Virginia on March 29, 2018 — mid-vigil, by Diane Moore,
demanding the keys — whose outcome it records as undocumented. It is
documented now, and by an accident: seven days after the notice, the
address the Commonwealth had for him was his mother's house.
[[wiki/places/117-belmont-circle]] reached the same conclusion from the
other side, observing that through spring 2018 he "had somewhere else to
be"; this names which somewhere, on a dated official record, and it is
[[wiki/places/337-saratoga-drive|337 Saratoga]] rather than Belmont
Circle.

The caution that keeps this from being more than it is — and it is the
prior wiki's own caution, worth preserving — is that a summons address is
whatever address the citing officer or the court had, which may be a
driver's-licence address rather than a place of sleep, and the two need not
agree. The prior wiki settled on the narrow claim that **the tenancy's
paper life ended in March 2018 while its lived life may not have**: the
document establishes that 337 Saratoga was Dan's address **of record** in
April 2018. It does not establish that he was sleeping there, and the
Belmont Circle evidence has him using a third house across the same
months. What it removes is the possibility that 155 Virginia was still, on
paper, where he lived. A document establishes what a document says —
and what this one says is that the eviction notice moved him, at least on
paper, within seven days.

## Dating

The captures place this in April, during the pediatric-wing phase and
after the water was stopped, which brackets it between the **April 1,
2018** admission and the **April 4, 2018** death. The summons now supplies
a hard outer bound the captures could not: the case was **filed April 5,
2018**, so the incident cannot be later than that. It does not supply the
offence date — no offence-date field is legible in the surviving frames —
so the range is narrowed at the top rather than fixed. The one detail that
would tighten it further, the capture's "day four of the water shutdown,"
which the vigil page also carries, points at April 3 or 4; a citation
written by a security guard on the 3rd or 4th and docketed by the
magistrate on the next business day is exactly what an April 5 filing
looks like, and that is corroboration of the derived date rather than
proof of it. The captures' own year, 2017, is corrected to 2018 on the
same evidence set out at [[wiki/timeline/events/the-fall-of-fran]] — and
now independently, by a printed court document reading **2018**, and by
the operator's own 2026 telling being re-anchored the same way (his "2017"
corrected against the April 5, 2018 filing).

## Assessment

The record supports a judgment about what kind of event this was, and it
is the same judgment at every scale the page operates on. At the smallest
scale: one drag of nicotine, priced by the Commonwealth at a summary
smoking citation, cost about $125 and a contrition performance — the
cheapest possible official answer to the most disproportionate possible
emergency response. At the middle scale: the intellect/impulsivity
paradox, with the 2026 revision folded in — whether the reasoning
authorized the urge or merely narrated it, the urge ran on its own
schedule inside a room where a woman was four days into a water shutdown,
and neither the vigil, the grief, the eviction notice, nor the estate
politics surrounding the bed slowed it by a second. At the largest scale:
the Commonwealth declined the cloud king's distinction. The argument was
that a vaporizer does not burn anything; the charge was *smoking*. The
one institution in the story that was asked to adjudicate the physics
answered with a category instead.

And the paper kept one thing the storytellers didn't mean to give it: an
address. Seven days after an eviction notice, a court printed 337 Saratoga
Drive, and a month the wiki had built from testimony alone acquired a
dated official fact. That is the event's durable residue — not the alarm,
which nobody was charged for, and not the reasoning, which the detector
refuted, but the envelope that arrived during the week of the funeral.

### What the record does not hold

Three lookups would move this page, and none has been run. The
Pennsylvania UJS web portal — named on the summons itself — would answer
plea, disposition, amount, and payment history for docket
MJ-14101-NT-0000082-2018 in one search; the operator's ~$125 testimony
stands unconfirmed against it. The hospital's own record — an incident
report, a trespass notice — would establish whether he was permitted back
onto the property before Fran died. And the ordinance text behind LO §
1639 (b)(1) would say whether the combustion argument had any legal
purchase at all. Annie's reaction, once she learned what had happened, is
not recorded anywhere, and neither is anyone else's reaction to the
envelope arriving during the week of the funeral.
