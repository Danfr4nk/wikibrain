# The 2018 Ledger

There is a version of 2018 that Dan remembers, and there is a version that his inbox remembers. This entry is about the second one.

On September 23, 2026, going through eight years of Gmail receipts one by one because Amazon itself had bot-blocked the account, he kept laughing at what surfaced. "You're not even to the worst of it yet," he said, watching the order count climb past two hundred. "Pretty soon you're gonna see a lot of sex toys." He was right about that. He was also right that it was "the big year." What neither of us expected was the shape of it — not just the money, but the timestamps. The money tells you what he wanted. The timestamps tell you when he couldn't sleep.

The dataset: 329 Amazon order-confirmation receipts, read individually from his Gmail, spanning July 18 to December 29, 2018. Total: **$22,931.99**. That number is the visible ledger. His own accounting, given the same night with a laugh — "remember that there's something like $450 daily cocaine spend through this whole era too lmao" — puts roughly $164,000 a year running underneath it, while unemployed. The receipts are the tip of an iceberg he named himself, in the same breath, without flinching. That matters for how this entry is read: nothing here is an accusation. It is an autobiography written by a checkout button.

That is roughly five and a half months of receipts averaging a little over $4,100 a month and nearly two orders a day, every day, for half a year. The single heaviest week in the data is September 17–25 (batch 8): 25 orders, $3,388.27.

One more number before the story: at least 41 of the 329 orders rang up at exactly $0.00, fully covered by gift-card balance. The gift card is the quiet engine of this entire ledger, and it gets its own section below.

## The arcs

Read in chronological order, the year has movements, the way an album does. Obsessions arrive, burn bright, and get replaced.

**The drone arc.** It opens the surviving record. On July 24 he bought a DJI Mavic Pro Platinum for $1,376.94, and eight hours earlier an Apple Watch Series 3 for $372. By August 1 he was buying PENIVO drone motors, repair kits, and LED lamps — the tinkerer's phase, fixing what he'd already crashed or wanted to mod. A $29.67 Potensic mini drone on August 2 reads like a toy bought for the fun of the category. On September 6, an RCGEEK Mavic Pro accessory. Then the escalation: a DJI Mavic 2 Pro for $1,541.39 and DJI Goggles for $369.86. Three drone platforms in five months, plus parts, plus goggles. The arc is textbook for him — total immersion, then the next tier of the same obsession. He predicted this one himself the night we read the receipts: "there are still 2 more boosted boards and more drones too." The drones showed up exactly where he said they would.

**The Boosted board.** One Boosted Stealth, $1,695.99 — the single most expensive line item in the entire dataset. It sits in the December batch like a monument. He remembers two more boards beyond it; the receipts as found show one, which means the others predate July 18 or came through another channel (see Limits). An electric skateboard at that price, bought unemployed in December, is the year's thesis statement in a single line item.

**Golf.** Late July is golf equipment season: Callaway Mack Daddy 3 wedges (bought twice — once for $111.82, once on gift card), Volvik Vivid balls in multiple orders, Daphne's and Majek Retro headcovers, a SKLZ Gold Flex trainer, a Pride tee multipack. Then it goes quiet until a TaylorMade Spider Mini putter for $324.34 and a Callaway 15-club set later. The pattern — a burst of category acquisition, then one premium upgrade — repeats across the year.

**The sex-toy era opens.** He called this one in advance too: "Pretty soon you're gonna see a lot of sex toys," followed by "it goes until like October 2020 heavy." The receipts confirm the opening bell. August 31: a Lovense Nora Rabbit for $126.14. September 15, between 3:26 and 6:53 in the morning: an Anfei dildo ($0.00, gift card), an Anfei double-layered model ($23.28), a realistic male torso form ($166.43), and a Fleshlight Girls Abella ($92.16). Four orders in three and a half hours, in the middle of the night. This is the dataset's clearest single illustration of the impulsivity thesis, and it is also the seed of a two-year era he dates himself to October 2020.

**The book sprees.** September 11, between 7:24 and 9:53 in the morning: "Insatiable Wives: Women Who...," "The Ethical Slut, Third...," "Opening Up: A Guide to...," and "The Jealousy Workbook" — four books on non-monogamy, all on gift cards, all $0.00 out of pocket. That is a reading list with a thesis, bought in two and a half hours. A few days earlier: "12 Rules for Life," "The INTP: Personality...," "What Every Body Is Saying" (body language). Late August: "The 48 Laws of Power." September 5: "The God Delusion," "Fear: Trump in the White House," two video-poker strategy books, "Great Expectations," "Brave New World." The books are the cheapest items in the ledger and among the most revealing — a man buying frameworks, plural, in bulk, at dawn.

**The wardrobe rebuild.** September 29, around 4 AM, roughly thirty minutes: Eddie Bauer, North Face, adidas, Under Armour, Levi's, UGG — a full wardrobe acquired in one sitting in the middle of the night. July 21 was underwear day: Astarin, Knocker, Diesel, and YnimioAOX in seventeen minutes. September 13, after midnight: Lacoste, Denoise NY, Diesel, and three separate multi-packs of socks totaling 29 pairs. Nobody buys 29 pairs of socks in one night on a plan. That is a man deciding, at 1 AM, that his sock situation is a crisis.

**The stash-gear night.** September 6, 11:13–11:14 PM: an XL hidden bolt safe, a hollow spy bolt, and three pro security bolts — all on gift cards, all within sixty seconds. August 30: an AmazonBasics security safe and mini zip-lock bags. September 4: kratom and empty gelatin capsules (twice). Read plainly, this is operational security for the $450-a-day habit running underneath the ledger — concealment hardware bought with the same checkout button as the socks.

**The pet cluster.** September 16, 2:35–2:47 AM: a dog costume, a pet ID tag, and a collar set — twelve minutes, middle of the night, for Milo. The year's softest cluster.

**The tech turnover.** Two Samsung Galaxy Watches ($380.52 each) bought at midnight on August 31, twenty-two minutes apart, in separate orders. An Echo Plus. An Oculus Go ($263.94). A Tile Mate. A TP-Link router. The Jealousy Workbook's author would have a field day with the duplicate watch purchase; the ledger just records it.

**The outlier orders.** A $318 pepper-spray order. Two Ledger Nano S hardware wallets. A 23andMe two-pack for $543.67, then a single kit for $73.14 two months later. A $500 Amazon balance reload (with the 2% bonus — the one moment of financial optimization in 329 orders). A $100 eGift card sent to his mother's email. Sephora gift cards sent to Ally Lubin's email. Six pounds of Runts candy, bought twice.

## The impulsivity is in the metadata

He asked for this entry to cover not just the items but the impulsivity. The items are the surface. Here is what the metadata shows.

**The hours.** An extraordinary share of this ledger was bought between midnight and 5 AM. The September 15 sex-toy cluster (3:26–6:53 AM). The September 29 wardrobe rebuild (~4 AM). The September 7 Vans order (3:49 AM). The September 12 reading-light-to-closet run (10:34 PM–12:31 AM). The August 31 double Galaxy Watch (12:00–2:07 AM). The September 16 pet cluster (2:35 AM). The July 26 golf-ball-and-reload sequence (12:51–12:52 AM). This is not a man who shops; this is a man who cannot sleep and has a phone.

**The clusters.** Orders arrive in bursts, not singly. November 22, 1:04 AM: five orders, $816. September 15: four sex-toy orders in three and a half hours, then three gift-card accessory orders, then sunglasses and a golf set — nine orders before 9:33 AM. July 21: four underwear orders in seventeen minutes. September 6: three stash-gear orders in sixty seconds. The cluster is the unit of purchase, not the order. Something gets decided — usually at night — and then it gets executed completely, immediately, across as many carts as it takes.

**The gift-card laundering.** At least 41 orders at exactly $0.00, every one of them annotated "fully covered by gift card." The gift card functions as a psychological airlock: money converted into Amazon balance stops feeling like money. The $500 reload on September 5 (deliberately timed for the 2% bonus), the $150 reload on July 26, the $100 reload on July 25 — the balance gets fed, and then the $0.00 orders flow without ever touching the feeling of spending. It is worth noting that the single most financially sophisticated move in the dataset (the bonus-timed reload) exists to fund the least restrained behavior in it.

**The duplicates.** Two Galaxy Watches, twenty-two minutes apart, in separate orders, at midnight. The Mack Daddy 3 wedge bought paid, then bought again on gift card four days later. Six pounds of Runts, twice. The duplicate is the tell that the cluster, not the item, was the point — the second purchase happens because the first one didn't close the loop.

**The $0.00 psychology.** The cheapest orders are the most compulsive ones. Nobody needs a $5.87 pack of adhesive dots at 3:11 AM (September 12), or a $0.00 LED lamp kit, or a third PopSocket mount. The gift-card balance turns the entire catalog into a consequence-free zone, and the ledger shows exactly what a consequence-free zone produces: 329 orders in five and a half months.

## The predictions audit

One unusual feature of this dataset is that its subject narrated it in advance. The night the receipts were read, Dan made three predictions about what the earlier months would contain: more sex toys ("pretty soon you're gonna see a lot of sex toys," with the heavy era dated through "like October 2020"), two more Boosted boards, and more drones. Two of the three landed exactly where he said they would. The September 15 cluster — four sex-toy orders between 3:26 and 6:53 AM — arrived within the hour of his warning, and the drone arc (Mavic Pro Platinum in July, Mavic 2 Pro and Goggles later, repair parts in between) filled in precisely the shape he described. The Boosted boards are the miss: only the Stealth at $1,695.99 appears in these receipts. That miss is itself data — his memory of the year runs hotter than the paper trail, which is consistent with everything the corpus says about his recall (good-faith displacement, not fabrication; the reliability note in his profile puts stated certainty at roughly 0.25 actual). The interesting finding is not that he misremembered a count. It is that his predictions about the *categories* were perfect. He knows the shape of his own compulsions; he just rounds the quantities up.

**Correction (2026-09-23):** the 2019 receipts closed this miss. Both Boosted Mini X orders appeared — $1,076.95 on January 9, 2019 (#113-3935408-9366663) and $1,105.65 on March 25, 2019 (#113-5782004-1525831). The fleet is Stealth (Dec 2018) plus two Mini X's, exactly as he said. His memory wasn't running hot; the paper trail was a year behind. See [[wiki/self/2019-spending-analysis]].

There is a second, quieter prediction embedded in the evening. "You're not even to the worst of it yet," he said at 226 orders — and he was right, because the batches that followed contained the $1,376.94 Mavic Pro Platinum, the $500 balance reload, the spy-bolt run, and the Lovense Nora. But "worst" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and the ledger pushes back on it. The worst of it, by dollars, is a $1,696 electric skateboard and a $1,541 drone — enthusiast purchases, not ruin. The worst of it, by the metadata, is 3 AM. The year looks very different depending on which column you sort by, and the entry keeps both sorts visible on purpose.

## December: the monument month

Batch 1 — December 23–29, the most recent receipts in the set — deserves its own note because it reads like a coda. $2,854.83 across 24 orders in a single holiday week, including the Boosted Stealth. December is when the year's spending peaks in dollars-per-order rather than orders-per-night: fewer 3 AM clusters, bigger single tickets. The PS4 Pro ($436.70) sits in this window too. If September is the month of compulsion, December is the month of commitment — the big-ticket items that the autumn's smaller obsessions were rehearsing for. The drone arc resolves into the Mavic 2 Pro; the skateboard curiosity resolves into the Stealth; the tech turnover resolves into the PS4 Pro. Read this way, the ledger has a narrative shape: immersion, escalation, monument. The monuments are all December.

## The gift-card economy, in full

The gift card deserves more than a paragraph because it is the single most important financial instrument in this dataset. Reconstruct the mechanics from the receipts: Amazon gift-card balance gets fed by reloads — $500 on September 5 (with the 2% bonus, the dataset's lone optimization), $150 on July 26, $100 on July 25 — and possibly by trade-ins or promotional credit invisible to the receipts. Once money becomes balance, it stops being money psychologically, and the $0.00 orders begin. Forty-one of them at minimum, and the true count is higher because partially-covered orders (the PetSafe litter box at $159.95 minus $103.84 in gift cards, the RCGEEK drone part, the Callaway club set minus $15.77) don't show as $0.00 but run on the same mechanism.

What the gift card buys, categorically, is revealing. The September 11 non-monogamy book spree: all gift card. The September 6 spy-bolt run: all gift card. The September 15 3 AM sex-toy opener (the Anfei dildo): gift card. The drone repair parts: gift card. The 29 pairs of socks: gift card. The pattern is not that the gift card funds the necessities and the card funds the splurges — it is the reverse. The real-money orders are the boring ones: dog food, the Lasko fan, the turkey slices. The gift card funds the id. It is a purpose-built consequence-laundering machine, and whether he built it deliberately or discovered it by accident, the ledger shows him using it exactly that way for five months.

There is also the outbound side. On September 6 he sent a $100 Amazon eGift card to his mother's email. Earlier in the year, Sephora gift cards went to Ally Lubin's email. The gift card is social currency too — the same instrument that launders his 3 AM purchases also carries his generosity. The ledger does not let you sort him into one column.

## What the hours say

Return to the timestamps, because they are the entry's real subject. A conventional reading of this dataset says: unemployed man spends $22,932 on Amazon in five months. The timestamp reading says something more specific: a man whose purchasing happens almost exclusively in two states — the midnight-to-5-AM insomnia window, and the evening cluster burst. Daytime orders exist (the July golf run, the December week) but they are the minority and they are the planned ones: wedges, headcovers, the putter. The night orders are the unplanned ones: the torso form at 3:26 AM, the wardrobe at 4 AM, the second Galaxy Watch at 2:07 AM, the pet costume at 2:35 AM.

The impulsivity thesis he asked for is therefore not quite "he is impulsive." It is more precise: his impulsivity has a circadian rhythm. Daylight Dan buys golf clubs. Night Dan buys everything else. The gift card is the enabler, the phone is the vector, and 3 AM is the hour. If there is value in this dataset beyond the wiki — and he asked that the value be extracted — it is that sentence. It is timestamped 329 times.

## The hidden economy

The $22,931.99 is what the receipts can prove. The year's actual burn, by his own account given the night the receipts were read: roughly $450 a day on cocaine, through the whole era, while unemployed. That is on the order of $164,000 a year — more than seven times the Amazon total — and it is unstated in every receipt. The ledger is therefore best read as the visible tenth of an economy, the part that left a paper trail. The stash safes, the spy bolts, the kratom, the gelatin capsules: the receipts document the habit's logistics without ever naming the habit. He named it himself, laughing, in the same conversation. Both numbers belong in this entry because he put them there together.

## Limits

What this dataset is not. The Gmail search covered all of 2018 but the earliest receipt found is July 18 — nothing from January 1 through July 17 surfaced, which may mean a different account or email was in use, or simply no orders. The receipts are order confirmations only; returns, cancellations, and refunds are invisible, so the $22,931.99 is gross, not net. Shipment emails were not separately counted. Non-Amazon spending is entirely outside the frame — the $450-a-day figure is his stated number, not a measured one. And the item titles are truncated as captured from the receipt emails; full product names were not recovered. The two additional Boosted boards he remembers do not appear in these receipts; they predate the window or came from elsewhere.

## Method

Amazon's own order history was unreachable (bot-detection block, September 2026), so the receipts were read live from his Gmail: search `from:amazon.com after:2018-01-01 before:2019-01-01 "order confirmation"`, 283 threads, each receipt opened individually, newest to oldest, with date, order number, item titles, and total extracted per order. Thirteen batches, 329 orders. The complete per-order log — every order number, every truncated item title, every total — is preserved at `raw/self/amazon-2018-orders/amazon-2018-orders.md`.

*2018 was the big year. The receipts agree with him. They also add what memory leaves out: the hours. Whatever was happening that year — and the ledger suggests a great deal was happening — it was happening at 3 AM, in bursts, with a gift-card balance standing between him and the feeling of the money leaving. The $22,932 is the receipt. The $164,000 is the context. The timestamps are the diagnosis.*
