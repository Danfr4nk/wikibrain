# Book Favorites

**120 books**, overwhelmingly non-fiction with heavy emphasis on politics, history, recent events, and power structures.

This reading list is a direct window into political and intellectual dimensions of the self model.

The author-level spread is not range. [[wiki/interests/favorites/eclecticism]] originally co-authored that reading; the 2026-09-01 rewrite retires it. Forty of 120 books are tagged `trump` or `jan-6` (thirty authors); twenty are tagged `roman-republic`, `ancient-history` or `caesar` (fourteen authors); the two sets do not overlap. One account per witness is all exhaustive coverage of a single event requires, which is why 85 of 98 authors appear once.

## Lede: the case file, not the library

On April 8, 2024, Dan posted that after finishing the @HistoriaCivilis Caesar/Octavian series he "started devouring the 'essential' books about the late Roman Republic" — then added the sentence that explains the whole shelf: "The books are wonderful, but none of them has come close to compiling the story in a way that has pulled me in like videos with squares." The next day he was live-tweeting Dan Carlin's "Death Throes of the Republic": Carlin "does a wonderful job of presenting the proto-Marxian narrative that underlies the whole era in a way that's both fair and fascinating," and the five-hour final episode was, per Dan, "the perfect introduction to the immortal story of Caesarian Rome." An earlier episode, he said, cleared up things he hadn't understood — he hadn't known why Pompey became the de facto leader of the anti-Caesarian faction until Carlin "lays out the amazing scope of his military achievements in a way I hadn't understood" (tweets, 2024-04-08/09, `raw/twitter/tweet-archive.csv`).

That two-day sequence is the whole method in miniature. Dan does not browse books; he closes cases. A subject arrives — a YouTube series, a presidency, an impeachment — and he reads *everyone*, one account per witness, until the event is covered from every angle that has a narrator attached. The Goodreads "read" shelf is not a library of tastes. It is a set of completed investigations. This is why the two largest clusters on it — Trump-era American politics and the late Roman Republic — are carried by forty-four different authors with zero overlap between the sets: exhaustive coverage of two events, not two interests in a reader's temperament. It is also why, in July 2025, he was uploading a PDF of the entire 120-book "read" shelf into ChatGPT (`raw/chatgpt/dfrank88-2025-08-05/.../chat.html`): the shelf is data to be worked, not a monument to be admired.

What follows is the shelf as evidence — the numbers from two captures of the same Goodreads account, the testimony about how and why he reads, and the contradictions between captures, which are themselves informative about how testimony decays around the objects he cares most about.

## The first obsession: the Trump era, exhaustively

The Trump-era cluster is the single largest subject on the shelf and the cleanest demonstration of the one-account-per-witness method. In the held export alone, the complete Michael Wolff series is present and read: *Fire and Fury* (5, read 2019-01-06), *Siege* (5), *Landslide* (5), *All or Nothing* (4, read 2025-05-20) — a full five-volume author read, the only complete-series read on the shelf. Three Jonathan Karl books (*Front Row at the Trump Show* 5, *Betrayal* 5, *Tired of Winning* 3), three Woodward books (*Fear* 4, *The Trump Tapes* 3, *War* 3), and the staffer memoirs that complete the witness list: *The Room Where It Happened* (Bolton, 3), *I'll Take Your Questions Now* (Grisham, 4), *A Sacred Oath* (Esper, 4), *Too Much and Never Enough* (Mary Trump, 4), *A Warning* (Anonymous, 3), *Unchecked* (Bade, 4, on the botched impeachments), *I Alone Can Fix It* (Leonnig/Rucker, 4), *Devil's Bargain* (Green, 3, on Bannon), *The Divider* (Baker/Glasser, 5), and *Fight* (Allen, 5, on the 2024 campaign). This is not a reader sampling the genre; it is a reader closing the file — every major narrator of the 2017–2025 period, insider and journalist, friendly and hostile, logged and rated.

Dan's own words confirm the reading was close, not ambient. On 2018-01-08 he posted a fine-grained observation from inside *Fire and Fury*: "Notably absent in #FireAndFury is Mike Pence. He's not completely without mention but his lack of presence in the larger, more defining moments described in the book is pretty telling. He is, if nothing else, diligently committed to his political future." That is a reader tracking who the narrator *doesn't* put in the room. Three months later, on 2018-04-17, he was publicly hyped for Comey's *A Higher Loyalty*: "been so excited for 'a higher loyalty' that i got clap'd by @AriMelber and his surgically-accurate unpacking of comey's partisan blunders. i'm no less hyped for the book, but i've got better perspective." The posture is fandom-grade engagement with political non-fiction — he anticipates releases the way other people anticipate albums.

The most revealing Trump-era artifact on the shelf is *The Chapo Guide to Revolution*, rated 5. On 2022-11-01 Dan posted: "One of my favorite things ever is @cushbomb performing the late obama years and 2016 election in the chapo book. Nothing else quite captures the fucking menacing and surreal energy of that moment like this." Matt Christman narrates the audiobook version of his own book — Dan heard it, not read it, and the testimony pins his five-star rating to the *performance* of a specific chapter, not the text's argument. The shelf's ratings are ratings of encounters, not just of books. (The audiobook vector is developed in [How he reads](#how-he-reads).)

The political shelf has a leftward tilt with a skeptic's appetite for the hostile witness. The 5-star *The Assassination of Julius Caesar* (Parenti) and the 5-star *Family of Secrets* (Baker, on the Bush dynasty) sit beside the 4-star *What's the Matter with Kansas?* (Thomas Frank), the 4-star *It's OK to Be Angry About Capitalism* (Sanders), the 4-star *A True History of the United States* (Sjursen), and — at zero stars, logged but unendorsed — *Das Kapital* and *The Conquest of Bread*. He keeps the canon on the shelf without inflating the rating: the zero-star entries read as "read it, granting it nothing." The Bush-era exposé cluster (*Family of Secrets* 5, *Hey Rube* 3, *The Forever War* 3, *The Afghanistan Papers* 5) and the conspiracy/exposé cluster (*Chaos* 3 on Manson/CIA, *CIA Rogues and the Killing of the Kennedys* 4, *The Politics of Fear* 3) extend the same institutional-skepticism line that the wiki's political profile already documents.

## The second obsession: Rome

The Roman cluster is the mirror image of the Trump cluster: same exhaustive method, different century, and here the trigger is documented in Dan's own words. The April 2024 sequence (Historia Civilis videos → "devouring the 'essential' books" → Carlin's "Death Throes") produced, in the held export, a five-star stack with no weak entries: *Rubicon* (Holland, 5, read 2024-04-07 — the day before the Historia Civilis tweet), *Caesar: Life of a Colossus* (Goldsworthy, 5, read 2024-07-31), *Cicero* (Everitt, 5, read 2024-12-21), *The Assassination of Julius Caesar* (Parenti, 5, read 2024-04-29), *Lives of the Noble Romans* (Plutarch, 5, read 2024-01-01), *Complete Works of Plutarch* (5, no date), *Cleopatra: A Life* (Schiff, 4, read 2024-09-21), and the one deliberate fiction entry, John Williams's *Augustus* (3, read 2024-07-31). Dan Carlin's *The End is Always Near* sits at 3 stars in the export (read 2024-09-21) — the book underwhelmed him even as the podcast's "Death Throes" episodes earned the "perfect introduction" praise in April.

Two things about this cluster matter. First, the politics are the point of contact, not the antiquity. Dan's praise for Carlin centers the "proto-Marxian narrative that underlies the whole era" — he reads the late Republic as a case study in how republics die, the same institutional-collapse lens he turns on the Trump era. The 5-star Parenti (a people's-history reading of Caesar's assassination) and the 5-star Everitt Cicero (a biography of "Rome's Greatest Politician") are the political-theory end of the cluster; the military-history end is Goldsworthy's *Caesar* and the Plutarch. Second, the cluster is *dated*: nearly all of it was read between January and December 2024. It is a bounded investigation with a start date (the Historia Civilis series, early April 2024) — exactly the forensic shape the Trump cluster has, and the opposite of a lifelong "interest in ancient history."

The shelf also shows the investigation spilling past the boundary: the want-to-read list (`wiki/interests/favorites/books/want-to-read`) holds Goldsworthy's *Punic Wars*, Everitt's *The Rise of Rome*, Strauss's *The War That Made the Roman Empire*, Beard's *The Roman Triumph*, Harris's *Dictator*, and Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar* — the Roman inquiry has a planned second wave.

## How he reads

The testimony is unusually specific about the mechanics, and it points one way: Dan is substantially an *audiobook* reader, and his printed-book apparatus (Kindle, ratings, challenges) serves the same binge logic.

- **Volume, stated plainly.** On 2023-02-02: "Politics has been so boring that I've plowed through a cumulative 92 hours of history audiobooks over the last 2 months using only my monthly audible credit." Ninety-two hours in two months, on one Audible credit — i.e., the monthly-credit titles plus library/borrowed overflow, history only, during a boring-news stretch. The reading expands to fill the attention vacuum.
- **Scribd over Audible.** On 2022-11-12: "Apropos of nothing and to the amusement of no one…I'm convinced that Scribd is far superior to Audible if you listen to a lot of audiobooks." He frames it as a power-listener's verdict: at his volume, the subscription-all-you-can-listen model beats the credit model. The 2018 SMS receipts corroborate the Audible side of the history — a "Get the Audible app!" promo text on 2018-09-18 and a Cash App "$15.85 at Audible" charge on 2018-12-12 (inbound transactional SMS, `raw/imessage/messages-part1-2011-2019.csv`).
- **Audiobook as the encounter.** The Chapo Guide five-star is explicitly for Christman's narration ("@cushbomb performing the late obama years and 2016 election in the chapo book"). The 2023-06-20 "top audiobook narration blunder" tweet — "this incredible moment from 'This Will Not Pass' by @jmart" — is a listener's complaint, not a reader's: he listens closely enough to catch the narrator's mistakes. *This Will Not Pass* (Martin/Burns) is itself a 2022 Trump-era book, so the audiobook vector runs straight through the political cluster.
- **Kindle.** Thirteen of the 63 held-export reads carry the "Kindle Edition" binding, and his Goodreads profile (July 2025 PDF session) has "Kindle Notes & Highlights" as a first-class section — he reads on Kindle and takes highlights. On 2018-08-22 he texted Annie: "But I'm gonna buy you a kindle" (Dan to Annie, iMessage) — the device was his default answer to someone else's reading problem, too.
- **Ratings as a compulsive log.** Every book is rated, including the zeros — *Das Kapital* (0), *The Conquest of Bread* (0), *Downtown: My Manhattan* (0) — and the single 1-star in the corpus, *None of This Rocks* (Joe Trohman's memoir, read Feb 2023). A reader who rates *Das Kapital* zero stars is not rating enjoyment; he is keeping a ledger. The 9 zero-ratings in the master list and the 3 in the held export are the ledger's "read, granting nothing" entries.
- **The gamified apparatus.** "Reading Challenge" and "Year in Books" appear on his own profile in the July 2025 session. He tracks annual totals — the shelf is quantified-self material, consistent with the 92-hours-in-two-months accounting.
- **The forward shelf.** Two books sit "currently-reading" in the held export, both added summer 2024: *The Situation Room* (Stephanopoulos) and *A New World Begins* (Popkin, on the French Revolution, tagged "massive"). The French Revolution book is the Roman inquiry's natural successor — republics, collapsing, in sequence.

## The Bacharach case: five years of the wrong title

The shelf's single most-discussed book is also its strangest data point. The held export logs exactly one Jacob Bacharach title — *The Doorposts of Your House and on Your Gates* (Liveright, 2017), shelved 2022-03-24, rated the maximum 5 stars — and *The Bend of the World* appears nowhere in the export or the master list. Yet Dan, per the record, has for years attributed the Virginia Avenue passage to *The Bend of the World* (2014), citing pages 227–228. Bacharach's own February 2021 message identified *"my Doorposts of Your House novel"* as the Uniontown book.

> **CORRECTED [2026-08-08]:** This paragraph previously read "Two of his books read and both top-rated," dated the novel to 2022, and treated *The Bend of the World* as a second five-star entry. Neither the Goodreads export nor `FAVS MASTERLIST.csv` contains *The Bend of the World* at all — 2022 is the shelving date, not the publication year, and only one Bacharach title has ever been logged. This matters beyond the count: *The Bend of the World* is the novel Dan names as the site of the [[wiki/places/155-virginia-ave]] coincidence chain, and the one book of Bacharach's he demonstrably read is the other one — which Bacharach himself identified in February 2021 as the Uniontown novel. The open contradiction is documented on [[wiki/people/jacob-bacharach]].

The kb layer reads this as a calibration datum for the whole testimony apparatus (`kb/interpretations/doorposts-novel.md`, L3, moderate confidence): the high-salience structure of the memory is vivid and stable — a novelist from his hometown wrote his street into a book — while the low-salience bibliographic label drifted and froze wrong, for five years, inside a system built to catch exactly this. The interpretation's stated falsifiers are worth keeping visible: a held message in which Dan names *Doorposts* correctly would collapse the wrong-title reading; a physical-copy check finding the passage on pages 227–228 of *The Bend of the World* would vindicate Dan's title and make Bacharach's own message the confusing one. Neither check has happened. The alternative the interpretation leaves open — that the five-star rating is for the *encounter* (hometown novelist, his street in print) rather than the text, and the title is a label for the event, not a bibliographic claim — fits everything else on this page about how Dan's ratings work.

One more note the record supports: the Bacharach five-star is the shelf's only fiction five-star, and the book is also the only title on the shelf with a documented real-world coincidence chain attached to it. The rating may be the least literary judgment in the corpus and the most personal.

## What is barely here

For a 120-book shelf, the absences are as structured as the presences:

- **Fiction is nearly absent.** In the held export's 63 reads, exactly three are fiction: *The Doorposts of Your House and on Your Gates* (5), *Brave New World* (Huxley, 4), *Augustus* (Williams, 3). No poetry, no short stories, no literary fiction beyond the hometown-novelist coincidence and a dystopia. The one fiction five-star is the personal one. Dan reads for the world, not for the sentence.
- **The zero-stars are a practice, not a verdict.** *Das Kapital*, *The Conquest of Bread*, *Downtown: My Manhattan* — logged at zero rather than left unrated. Against the music corpus, where most tracks are unrated, the book shelf's complete rating coverage (every book rated, even at zero) marks reading as a deliberately *accounted* activity.
- **The pre-2022 reading life is thin on the record.** The master list's earliest date-read is 2019 (1 book); the held export's earliest dated read is 2019-01-06 (*Fire and Fury*). Whether he read little before 2022 or simply started logging then is undocumented — the Goodreads account's date-added histogram (2022: 46–28 across captures) suggests the logging practice itself began in 2022. Nothing on the shelf testifies to a childhood or college reading life.
- **The want-to-read shelf is a map of intent, not consumption.** The older DAN_COMP mine logged 149 to-read titles; the held export logs 37 to-read plus 2 currently-reading. Both captures agree on the shape — same obsessions (Trump-era, Rome, intelligence/conspiracy history), plus a distinct NYC-history thread (*The Power Broker*, *Boss Tweed*, *The Bowery*, *St. Marks Is Dead*, *Lost New York*) that barely appears in the read list. See [[wiki/interests/favorites/books/want-to-read]] for the full list. Intent and consumption have the same geometry.

## Assessment

The book shelf is the most forensic of Dan's taste corpora and the least temperamental. Where the music shelf is sprawling and the film shelf is social, the books are an instrument: each title is a witness statement, each rating a verdict, each cluster a closed investigation. The 2023–2024 binge — forty-four authors across two subjects, a 2022 shelving wave read through in a single long push, 92-hour audiobook months — is the reading equivalent of the corpus work he does on everything else: total coverage of the event, one account per witness, then move on. The testimony interleaves cleanly with the ratings (the Chapo five-star is for a narration; the *Fire and Fury* close read precedes its five-star), which is why the shelf reads as honest data rather than signaling.

Two caveats bound the page. First, the captures disagree on counts and on several ratings, and the older capture's sources are unresolved — the master-list figures are carried as the older capture's testimony, the export's as the held capture's, and neither is silently merged into the other. Second, the shelf's most personal entry — the Bacharach five-star — may be the least literary judgment in it, and the wrong-title problem it carries is the wiki's cleanest specimen of testimony decay: five years, a fact he cared about, inside a system built to catch it. The reading list is a window into the political and intellectual dimensions of the self model, as the original page said. The 2026-10-07 pass adds: it is also a window into how the model's memory fails — preserving the event, losing the label, and rating the encounter five stars either way.
