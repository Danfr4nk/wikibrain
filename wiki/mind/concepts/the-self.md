# The Self

The Self is Jung's name for the total personality — the whole of which the ego is only a part. "I have suggested calling the total personality which, though present, cannot be fully known, the self. The ego is, by definition, subordinate to the self and is related to it like a part to the whole." It is at once a **centre** and a **totality**: "a psychic totality and at the same time a centre, neither of which coincides with the ego but includes it, just as a larger circle encloses a smaller one." *Aion* (Vol 9-II) is its central text; everything else in the psychology orbits it.

The concept is the answer to a structural problem. If the psyche is larger than consciousness — and the whole of Jung's psychology says it is — then there must be a centre of the *whole*, not just a centre of consciousness. The ego is the centre of the field of consciousness. The Self is the centre of the totality, conscious and unconscious together.

## Ego and Self: part to whole

The distinction is the most violated in popular Jung, so Jung states it with unusual bluntness. The ego is "the complex factor to which all conscious contents are related," "the centre of the field of consciousness" — and, as criterion: "no content can be conscious unless it is represented to a subject." It rests on somatic and psychic bases, conscious and unconscious, and is "only relatively stable."

The Self enfolds it. In Vol 7 Jung gives the image that stuck: the ego revolves around the Self "as the earth revolves round the sun." The Self is "the mid-point of the personality," "a virtual centre midway between conscious and unconscious, supraordinate to the ego, embracing both." Intellectually it is "no more than a psychological concept, a construct that serves to express an unknowable essence which we cannot grasp as such" — "the God within us," a transcendental postulate required to formulate the empirical processes, not a metaphysical claim.

Two consequences follow. First, the Self is **older and younger than consciousness**, enfolding the ego in time as well as space: "the unconscious is the mother of consciousness." Second, it is **felt as object, not subject**: "felt empirically not as subject but as object." One does not *have* a Self the way one has an ego; one *encounters* it — in dreams, in mandalas, in the autonomy of unconscious processes. Because of its unconscious component it "can only be partially expressed by human figures; the other part of it has to be expressed by objective, abstract symbols": circle, sphere, square, quaternity, the mandala.

## Complexio oppositorum: the Self contains the opposites

"Like all archetypes, the self has a paradoxical, antinomial character. It is male and female, old man and child, powerful and helpless, large and small. The self is a true *complexio oppositorum*." Jung's conclusion in *Aion*: "we have to acknowledge that the self is a complexio oppositorum precisely because there can be no reality without polarity."

This is why wholeness is not goodness. The Christ-image, Jung argues, "exemplifies the archetype of the self" — but only its light half: "The Christ-image is as good as perfect (at least it is meant to be so), while the archetype (so far as known) denotes completeness but is far from being perfect." The dark half has its own figure: the Antichrist as "the shadow of the self, namely the dark half of the human totality." Individuation therefore aims at **completeness, not perfection** — "an approximate state of wholeness that lacks perfection," in which the individual "must suffer from the opposite of his intentions for the sake of his completeness."

## The Self and the God-image

At the empirical level Jung refuses to separate them: mandala symbolism is "at the same time a God-image," and there is an "impossibility of distinguishing empirically between 'self' and 'God.'" "As the highest value and supreme dominant in the psychic hierarchy, the God-image is immediately related to, or identical with, the self, and everything that happens to the God-image has an effect on the latter."

At the methodological level he draws the line hard: "Psychological truths are not metaphysical insights." Psychology "can only establish that the symbolism of psychic wholeness coincides with the God-image, but it can never prove that the God-image is God himself, or that the self takes the place of God." The distinction is load-bearing for the whole wiki: the Self is treated here as a psychological concept, full stop.

## The mandala: the Self's signature

The Self announces itself in a characteristic image: the mandala — "the premonition of a centre of personality… to which everything is related, by which everything is arranged, and which is itself a source of energy." Mandalas appear spontaneously "in conditions of psychic dissociation or disorientation" as "an attempt at self-healing on the part of Nature": "the severe pattern imposed by a circular image of this kind compensates the disorder and confusion of the psychic state — namely, through the construction of a central point to which everything is related."

The formal grammar is consistent: circle, quaternity ("the 'squaring of the circle'… could even be called the archetype of wholeness"), centre as sun or star, rotation. Triadic forms are "disturbed totality pictures." And the therapeutic effect belongs to spontaneity alone: "Nothing can be expected from an artificial repetition or a deliberate imitation of such images."

## Inflation: the danger at the centre

"The great psychic danger which is always connected with individuation, or the development of the self, lies in the identification of ego-consciousness with the self." When the ego mistakes the Self's energy for its own — or, conversely, psychologizes the unconscious out of existence to keep the ego supreme — the result is **inflation**: "Inflation magnifies the blind spot in the eye, and the more we are assimilated by the projection-making factor, the greater becomes the tendency to identify with it."

The prophylaxis is discrimination: "fixing reasonable boundaries to the ego and granting the figures of the unconscious — the self, anima, animus, and shadow — relative autonomy and reality (of a psychic nature)." For the over-conscientious ego, Jung prescribes the paradoxical cure: "the presumption of the ego can only be damped down by moral defeat."

## Conflicts in the record

- **Psyche-and-matter speculation.** In *Aion* XIV Jung reflects that "psyche cannot be totally different from matter, for how otherwise could it move matter?" — flagged in the extraction notes as low-confidence, "extremely hypothetical" by Jung's own admission. It is recorded here as reflection, not doctrine.
- **Self vs self (naming).** The miners proposed both `the-self.md` and `self.md`; the Vol 9-II treatment (totality-centre, complexio oppositorum, God-image) is the definitional home and this page consolidates it. Facet proposals (neutral self/quis-quid, self-as-centre-of-gravity) fold in as enrichment, not separate pages.
- **Completeness vs perfection.** Jung's insistence that the archetype "denotes completeness but is far from being perfect" stands in deliberate tension with the Christian perfect-image tradition he analyzes. The tension is the point; it is not resolved here.
