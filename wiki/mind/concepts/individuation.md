# Individuation

Individuation is Jung's name for the process by which a person becomes a psychological individual — "a separate, indivisible unity or 'whole.'" It is the master developmental process of the psychology: the synthetic work of integrating the unconscious into consciousness, "making as fully conscious as possible the constellated unconscious contents" and "synthetizing them with consciousness through the act of recognition." In Vol 6, its definitional home, Jung calls it "the development of the psychological individual as a being distinct from the general, collective psychology… a process of differentiation, having for its goal the development of the individual personality": "separation and differentiation from the general and a building up of the particular."

The Vol 7 formulation is the most quoted: individuation is "coming to selfhood" or "self-realization" — "the process by which a man becomes the definite, unique being he in fact is," the "production and unfolding of the original, potential wholeness." It "follows the natural course of life — a life in which the individual becomes what he always was."

## The conditions

Jung sets strict gates, and they rule out the two most common misreadings. First, **collective adaptation comes first**: "Before it can be taken as a goal, the educational aim of adaptation to the necessary minimum of collective norms must first be attained." You do not get to skip the persona; you have to earn the right to outgrow it. Second, **individuation is not isolation**: "the process of individuation must lead to more intense and broader collective relationships and not to isolation." The individual standpoint "is not antagonistic to [the collective norm], but only differently oriented" — and "the individual way can, by definition, never be a norm."

Third, it is **not individualism and not egotism**. Individualism is "deliberately stressing… some supposed peculiarity"; individuation "aims at a living co-operation of all factors" and is "the better and more complete fulfilment of the collective qualities of the human being." The particularity it builds up is "not a particularity that is sought out, but one that is already ingrained in the psychic constitution." It cannot be achieved by conscious intention — which "always leads to a typical attitude" — and the persona must first be dissolved: individuation means "divesting the self of the false wrappings of the persona and of the suggestive power of primordial images."

## The order: shadow, syzygy, Self

The process has a fixed sequence. "The integration of the shadow, or the realization of the personal unconscious, marks the first stage in the analytic process, and without it a recognition of anima and animus is impossible." The shadow is the "apprentice-piece"; the anima is the "master-piece"; behind the anima stands the wise old man; at the centre, the Self.

The relation that must hold between conscious and unconscious throughout is not conquest but negotiation: "a fair fight with equal rights on both sides… open conflict and open collaboration at once." Out of that conflict the unconscious produces the irrational third — the transcendent function, "the union of opposites" — and "between them the patient iron is forged into an indestructible whole, an 'individual.'"

The Vol 11 gloss is the shortest: individuation is "becoming whole," and wholeness "requires staking the whole being" — the shift of the centre of personality from ego to Self.

## The mechanism and the goal

The engine of the process is the **transcendent function**: from the collision of opposites, where consciousness knows only *tertium non datur*, "the unconscious psyche always creates a third thing of an irrational nature, which the conscious mind neither expects nor understands." Its symbols are mandalas and quaternities; its images are the child (futurity, "potential future"), the coniunctio (the nuptial union of opposites), the squaring of the circle.

The goal is the **Self** — "a shifting of the centre of personality from the ego to the self" — and the telos is completeness, not perfection: "The Christ-image is as good as perfect… while the archetype (so far as known) denotes completeness but is far from being perfect." Vol 16 adds the lived form: becoming "what one really is" through assimilation of the unconscious, an at-one-ment with oneself and, through that, with humanity.

## The dangers

Two are named. The first is **inflation**: "the great psychic danger which is always connected with individuation… lies in the identification of ego-consciousness with the self." The second is the collective undertow: individuation "creates a horror vacui in the collective layer and is only too likely to collapse under the impact of the collective forces of the psyche." Withdrawing projections constellates the deep water — which is why Jung calls the whole opus "an *opus contra naturam*," a work against nature, against the collective gravity that wants every difference reabsorbed.

And the Vol 9-I honesty clause: "Nobody has ever been able to tell the story of the whole way… for it is not the story-teller but death who speaks the final 'consummatum est.'"

## Conflicts in the record

- **Source artifact in the definitional home.** The EPUB HTML of Vol 6's "Definitions" (part0261.html) contains literal `<redacted>` artifacts, notably in the SOUL-IMAGE entry. The extraction notes verify no quote used on this page crossed a redaction, but any future quotation from part0261 should be checked against print.
- **Timing of the process.** Vol 7 places individuation proper "at the afternoon of life" for many; Vol 9-I treats it as the standing synthetic task of analysis at any stage. Jung does not resolve whether the afternoon-of-life framing is a rule or a typical case; both formulations are recorded.
- **Individual vs individuality.** The Vol 7 addendum distinguishes them carefully: individuality is "that which is peculiar and unique in a given combination of collective psychological elements," while the individual remains "an indispensable link in the social structure." Conflating the two is the individualism error Jung warns against.
