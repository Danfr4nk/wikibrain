# Archetype
_Layer: interpreted/concept — one mechanism, defined here. The evidence it draws on lives in the linked self/ exhibits, which it cites rather than restating._

The archetype is Jung's name for the contents of the collective unconscious: archaic, primordial types — "universal images that have existed since the remotest times," "definite forms in the psyche which seem to be present always and everywhere." The term is old — Philo Judaeus used it of the God-image in man, Irenaeus, the Corpus Hermeticum ("archetypal light"), Dionysius the Areopagite — and Jung adopts it as "an explanatory paraphrase of the Platonic *eidos*." What is new is the psychological claim: these are not philosophical abstractions but **empirical structures of the psyche**, as regular as instincts and as inescapable.

Everything load-bearing about the concept is in one distinction: the archetype is not the image. The archetype *as such* is empty and formal; the archetypal *image* is what appears when that form meets an individual consciousness. Confuse the two and the whole psychology collapses into mythology-worship.

## The definition: form without content

Jung's mature definition is precise and easily misquoted. The archetype is "in itself empty and purely formal, nothing but a *facultas praeformandi*, a possibility of representation which is given a priori." What is inherited is not the image but the *possibility of the image* — "not inherited ideas but inherited possibilities of ideas."

His analogy is the crystal: the archetype is like the axial system of a crystal, which preforms the crystalline structure without itself having material existence. "There are as many archetypes as there are typical situations in life" — birth, death, motherhood, the hero's ordeal, the wise elder — and each begins as "forms without content, representing merely the possibility of a certain type of perception and action."

In their quiescent, unprojected state archetypes "have no exactly determinable form" — they are "an indefinite structure which can assume definite forms only in projection." They are, in Jung's other formulation, "the unconscious images of the instincts themselves… patterns of instinctual behaviour": the image-side of instinct, which is why they carry instinct's compulsiveness. When an archetype corresponding to a situation is activated, it produces a compulsiveness "which, like an instinctual drive, gains its way against all reason and will" — or, blocked, a neurosis.

## The load-bearing distinction: archetype vs archetypal image

"The term 'archetype' thus applies only indirectly to the *représentations collectives*," Jung writes, "since it designates only those psychic contents which have not yet been submitted to conscious elaboration and are therefore an immediate datum of psychic experience." Myth, fairytale, dogma, esoteric teaching — these are archetypes *after* consciousness has worked them over. "The archetype is essentially an unconscious content that is altered by becoming conscious and by being perceived, and it takes its colour from the individual consciousness in which it happens to appear."

This is why the concept survives the obvious objection ("but myths differ across cultures"). The *form* is universal; the *image* is local, personal, elaborated. The mother archetype is everywhere; the Madonna and Kali are its elaborations. The distinction also sets the therapeutic task: analysis does not worship the image, it traces the image back to the form — and then forward again, consciously.

## The roster

Jung names the principal archetypes across the Collected Works. The order of encounter in analysis is fixed: **shadow** first (the denied side, nearest consciousness), then **anima/animus** (the contrasexual soul-figure — "the archetype of life itself"), then the **wise old man** (the archetype of meaning, "the pre-existent meaning hidden in the chaos of life"). Behind the shadow stands the anima; behind the anima, the wise old man — a stratification Jung states explicitly.

Others: the **mother** ("the loving and the terrible mother" — the matrix, "the form into which all experience is poured"); the **child** (futurity, "potential future," the uniting symbol); the **trickster** ("a collective shadow figure, a summation of all the inferior traits of character in individuals"); the **maiden/Kore**; and at the centre, the **Self** — the archetype of wholeness itself, toward which the whole process moves.

There is also a second class: the **archetypes of transformation**, which "are not personalities, but are typical situations, places, ways and means, that symbolize the kind of transformation in question" — the night sea journey, the descent, the alchemical *opus*. "No archetype can be reduced to a simple formula. It is a vessel which we can never empty, and never fill."

## Constellation: how archetypes activate

An archetype is constellated — energized, brought into operation — when outer situation and inner readiness coincide. The activated archetype then behaves like an instinct: it fascinates, compels, and organizates perception around itself. "Everything the anima touches becomes numinous — unconditional, dangerous, taboo, magical." The danger is identification: "If one can possibly avoid it, one ought never to identify with an archetype." Identification produces inflation and possession — the ego mistaking the archetype's energy for its own.

At collective scale the same mechanism goes historical. When the archetype corresponding to a situation is activated in a whole people, "those explosive and dangerous forces hidden in the archetype come into action, frequently with unpredictable consequences." Jung's example, written as it happened, is the Nazi revival of archaic Germanic symbols.

## Conflicts in the record

- **The 1907 symbol/allegory reversal.** In Vol 3 (1907) Jung treated the symbol as a deficit-product of reduced attention — a position he explicitly superseded. The mature doctrine (Vol 9-I and after) treats the symbol as the genuine, inexhaustible expression of the archetype: "genuine symbols precisely because they are ambiguous, full of half-glimpsed meanings, and in the last resort inexhaustible." The early position is recorded here as superseded, not as current doctrine.
- **"Inherited" language.** Jung's "owe their existence exclusively to heredity" sits uneasily beside his own insistence that archetypes are "empty and purely formal." The mining pass records both statements without reconciling them; the formal reading (possibilities, not contents) is the one Jung defends when pressed.
- **Coinage credit.** The extraction notes locate the term's first Jungian definition in Vol 5 (part0211.html). The definitional home of the mature concept is Vol 9-I.
