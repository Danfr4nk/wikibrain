# Trump Was Always Going To Win: the claim, audited

On November 12, 2024 — one week after the election — Dan Frank published the tenth and final post of his Substack, [Committee to Re-elect the President](wiki/self/substack/committee-to-re-elect-the-president.md), under the title "Trump Was Always Going To Win." It is a victory lap written by a man who is not celebrating. The audit below takes the post's central claim — "Over two years ago, I concluded that a Trump victory in 2024 had become the most likely outcome" — and checks it against the author's own archive, post by post, quote by quote. The nomination call survives the audit in full. The general-election call does not survive in the form it's remembered in. This is the reliability pattern made flesh: analysis reliable, autobiography of the analysis drifting — good-faith misremembering that flatters the teller, exactly as documented in his own veracity ledger ("trust the corpus over me on years").

For the publication itself, see [Committee to Re-elect the President](wiki/self/substack/committee-to-re-elect-the-president.md); for the full critical reading of all ten posts, [the critical reading](wiki/self/substack/committee-to-re-elect-the-president-critical-reading.md); for the frame the whole project runs inside, [Hell World](wiki/mind/concepts/hell-world-frame.md); for how this sits among every scored prediction, [the scoreboard](wiki/mind/synthesis/the-scoreboard.md); for the founding thesis the receipts point back to, [The DeSantis Delusion](wiki/mind/politics/desantis-delusion-thesis.md).

## 1. The post

"Trump Was Always Going To Win" (canonical URL slug: `trump-redux`) went up at 03:38 UTC on November 12, 2024 — about 3:38 a.m. Eastern on a Tuesday, a week to the day after the election, written in the small hours by a man who could not sleep. It runs 1,002 words, the shortest full essay in the corpus after the 38-word numerological opener. The subtitle does the emotional work the title refuses to: *"It's the down-ballot destruction that is so shocking and terrifying."* The title claims inevitability; the subtitle admits the thing he did not foresee. Read the two together and you have the whole post in eleven words.

The voice is audibly different from the May 2023 launch burst. Across the full ~7,200-word corpus there are exactly three profane instances — "clusterfuck" (the DeSantis launch), "fucking" ("I find the whole thing to be very fucking weird," of RFK Jr.), "batshit" ("absolutely, unequivocally batshit insane," of the coming election) — and this post contains zero of them. The syntax flattens: longer sentences, fewer one-line paragraph punches, no Poll Watch bullet energy, no jokes. Where the launch-phase Dan wrote "Strap in" and meant it as a thrill ride, the November Dan writes "Brace yourself for… something" and the ellipsis is the sound of a man who has run out of apocalypse jokes. The [Hell World frame](wiki/mind/concepts/hell-world-frame.md) works as comedy when the doom is prospective; once the worst reading comes true, the frame stops being funny and the register knows it. The confidence of May 2023 has curdled into dread confirmed.

Structurally the post is a triptych: the claim and its receipts (the primary was a fait accompli), a long 2020-Democratic-primary recap doing argumentative work as the origin story of Biden's vulnerability, and a catalogue of what the victory revealed (lawfare backfiring, the down-ballot sweep, the shattered opposition). It closes by converting the Substack into a watchtower: "This may become an outlet for tracking what happens next." It never posted again — the thirteenth month of silence became permanent.

## 2. The claim

The opening paragraph is the whole case in miniature:

> "Over two years ago, I concluded that a Trump victory in 2024 had become the most likely outcome of the coming election. At the time, media focus had spent months fixated on the (yet unannounced) DeSantis candidacy and its favorable position to overcome Donald Trump in the primary races."

The receipts he cites are the DeSantis Delusion posts of May 2023 — he links directly to "The DeSantis Delusion" (2023-05-23) on the word "obvious": "It quickly became obvious that nothing short of a miracle would prevent Trump from winning the nomination." Note what is being claimed and what the receipts actually support. The 2023 post says, with total confidence, that *the nomination* was a fait accompli. The 2024 post's opening sentence claims he concluded *a Trump victory in 2024* — the general election — was the most likely outcome. Those are different claims, and the receipts only cover one of them.

The post itself almost admits this. Two paragraphs after the victory claim comes the tell, stated plainly: "While the primary was simple math, the general election presented more complex variables." Then, in place of a general-election argument from 2023, the post narrates a process of *growing* confidence: "But none of this provided me enough reason to believe Trump would easily win in 2024. The odds started to tilt in Trump's favor as Biden's vulnerabilities became more glaring… With every development, my confidence grew that a second Trump term was imminent."

Read those sentences at face value and the claim dissolves into its components: an early, total-confidence primary call; a general-election lean that hardened over time. That is not what the opening sentence says. The opening sentence compresses "I nailed the primary, the polls leaned Trump, my confidence grew all cycle" into "I concluded two years ago." This is the audit's sharpest finding, and the rest of this article is the receipts.

## 3. The audit, stated fairly

Start with what is airtight, because it is genuinely elite.

**The nomination call was total-confidence and correct.** "The DeSantis Delusion" (2023-05-23) argued the GOP primary was a fait accompli on mechanism, not vibes: Trump's ~35% floor of immovable base support plus his documented willingness to sabotage any Republican who defied him made him unbeatable in a party where elections are won by razor-thin margins. "This isn't a prediction. This is a precedent," he wrote — and he was right: DeSantis dropped out after Iowa in January 2024, and not a single challenger came within twenty points. In May 2023 the media consensus, which he documents ("From Fox to MSNBC, DeSantis was cast as a rising force in GOP"), had DeSantis as a live threat backed by Murdoch's money. Dan called it against the full weight of that consensus, 890 words, specific, mechanistic, early. It is the best call in the corpus and the one he is entitled to keep forever.

**The general-election call in the contemporary text was explicitly hedged.** This is where the memory outruns the record. The May–June 2023 posts are careful to the point of modesty about the general:

- *POLL WATCH #1* (2023-05-26), the data-blogger installment, opens its methodology with a flat disclaimer: "it's still too early to look at polls with any reasonable amount of confidence in their individual results… it's worth the effort to start following now as the trends begin to emerge." That is a man tracking a trend, not a man who has concluded.
- *The Keys of Political Predictions* (2023-06-02), his explainer of Allan Lichtman's 13 Keys, relays a framework whose then-current application "will favor Biden" — and he relays it without suppressing it. A man who had already concluded a Trump victory was the most likely outcome does not publish a Biden-favoring forecast framework straight, no spin.

And here is the part of the contemporary record that gives the "directionally right" verdict its real weight: the Poll Watch data-blogger strand. *POLL WATCH #1* (2023-05-26) logged Trump leading Biden by +1.4 in the RealClearPolitics average; a week later *POLL WATCH #2* had it at +1.8, with the explicit read that the indictments hadn't shed as much support as the consensus assumed. That read held for the entire cycle — the indictments never dented him, and he won the popular vote. So the directional lean wasn't vibes; it had a data trail, published in real time, with the hedges attached. What the November post does is take that hedged, evidence-tracked lean and remember it as a conclusion. The evidence was real. The "I concluded" is the retrofit. Credit the tracking; press the memory.

Neither post is embarrassed by this. They are a forecaster doing the work in real time: the nomination is decided (fait accompli), the general is open (track the trends, here's a framework). The November 2024 post retrofits certainty backward onto the earlier text — takes the total-confidence tone of the primary call and pastes it onto the general-election outcome. It is directionally right: the polls did lean Trump, Biden's vulnerabilities were exactly the ones he'd named (age, cognition — see the Dem-primary post), and his confidence did grow as events confirmed the read. But the *certainty* was acquired later than remembered.

**The timeline is wrong too.** "Over two years ago" (written November 2024) describes May 2023. May 2023 → November 2024 is eighteen months — a six-month date displacement on his own archive, by the man whose standing rule is "trust the corpus over me on years." The corpus, consulted, says eighteen months. This is the documented pattern at work: stated certainty ≈ 0.25 actual; testimony skews toward good-faith misremembering (date displacement), not dishonesty. Nothing here is fabricated. Everything here flatters the teller. That distinction is the whole reliability note, and this post is its clearest exhibit.

**Net audit:** great primary call, stated with total confidence and proven right on mechanism; directionally-right general lean, stated in real time with explicit hedges; certainty and timestamp both inflated in retrospect. Credit the elite call fully; press the retrofit precisely; keep the tone forensic, not cruel. He was more right than almost anyone writing in May 2023. He was not as right as the November post says.

## 4. The 2020-primary recap as argumentative work

The center of the post is a long recap of the 2020 Democratic primaries — twenty Democrats entering, Bernie Sanders gaining momentum and leading the polls, Biden stumbling early, the Obama-coordinated moderate dropout before Super Tuesday, James Clyburn breaking tradition to endorse in South Carolina, Liz Warren splitting the progressive vote, COVID arriving and anointing Biden the "safe" choice while Sanders's agenda was framed as utopian and unelectable, Biden consolidating through triangulation and running a pandemic-era "no-show" campaign to barely beat "a president who had bungled the pandemic response and stoked racial animus."

On the surface this is throat-clearing. It is doing real argumentative work: it is the origin story of the vulnerability the whole post diagnoses. The 2020 primary, in this telling, was the moment the Democratic Party chose the safe, triangulated, donor-acceptable candidate over the movement candidate — and 2024 is the bill coming due for that choice. Biden "barely" won in 2020 despite every structural advantage (pandemic bungling, expanded mail-in voting, a culturally charged moment), and the recap's quiet implication is that a barely-won triangulation had no reserves for a second run. Every Biden liability the post goes on to name — inflation, immigration, the midterms loss of the House, age curdling from "Biden gaffes" into "serious cognitive decline," "a picture… of a president literally incapable of campaigning" — reads as the delayed detonation of a bomb planted in 2020.

The recap is also doing defensive work for the author. If 2024 was the bill coming due for 2020's triangulation, then the 2023 posts' hedged general-election read is retroactively justified: the variables *were* complex, because the outcome depended on how long Biden's team could keep a visibly declining president upright. "With every development, my confidence grew" is the honest version of the story — confidence tracking evidence in real time — and it sits one paragraph away from the inflated version ("Over two years ago, I concluded"). The post contains both the retrofit and its own correction. The audit just has to notice which one has the receipts.

## 5. The lawfare reading

The post's account of the indictments is its strongest analytical passage:

> "Trump's criminal cases, rather than hobbling him, paradoxically strengthened his base. Each indictment took on the appearance of 'lawfare,' with Trump's supporters viewing the cases as politically motivated persecution. Ironically, the weakest case — the Stormy Daniels hush-money trial — was the only one to reach trial before the election, solidifying MAGA's belief in a DOJ targeting Trump for purely political reasons."

Two things to note. First, the irony is genuinely sharp: of four criminal cases, the one that reached trial was the tawdriest and thinnest, which meant the base's persecution narrative got its day in court wearing the prosecution's weakest suit. The mechanism is clean — perceived persecution converts legal jeopardy into loyalty fuel — and he names it without flinching at "despite the mountain of evidence behind each case," refusing the easy version of the argument where the cases were all nothing.

Second, and this is where the audit gives him the credit the November post almost withholds from itself: in June 2023 this was still an open question, and he left it open. "The Horserace (From Hell)" (2023-06-09), written the night the federal indictment news broke, framed it exactly as the live fork it was: "will the broad swaths of GOP voters rally behind Trump or prefer a candidate who isn't facing time in federal prison?" They rallied. He read it right — not in June 2023, when he was asking, but as the cycle answered the question, exactly the way "with every development, my confidence grew" says. The lawfare call is the honest version of the victory claim: a question posed in real time, answered by events, incorporated into the model. ✅ Hit — and earned the slow way.

## 6. What genuinely surprised him

For all the claimed foresight, the post is explicit about what it did not see coming — and the subtitle puts it first: the down-ballot destruction. Not Trump winning; the GOP winning *everything around him*.

"Trump, a candidate known for his lack of impulse control and minimal grasp of history or governance, now presides over a Republican-controlled Congress filled with loyalists. The few Republicans willing to oppose him are gone." The mechanism is consolidation: Trump doesn't just win, he *keeps*. Paul Ryan exited; the McConnell era ended; what remains is a party "entirely under his control, with a judiciary he helped shape." And the sentence that gives the subtitle its terror: "Most alarming, Trump now holds a popular vote victory and the mandate that comes with it. He can claim to be enacting the will of the American voter, and in a sense, he would be right."

"He would be right" is the bravest sentence in the post. The hell-world frame licenses pessimism as realism — the worst reading is the correct one — but this goes past pessimism into concession: the mandate is real, the claim to the voter's will is legitimate, and the man making it is, "by any reasonable standard, unqualified." The tension between those two facts is the whole dread of the piece. A stolen-election narrative would be easier to live with; an earned mandate for an unqualified man is worse, because there is nothing to appeal.

The Democratic side of the ledger gets the postmortem paragraph: the party "lies shattered, devoid of credibility or a foundation for 'resistance,'" the Harris campaign "widely regarded as an embarrassment," and the infighting already running on two rails — moderates arguing she was too "woke," alienating Hispanic and blue-collar voters; progressives countering that lukewarm stances on Palestine and immigration made the Democrats "Republican-lite," alienating the base and disincentivizing young voters. He doesn't adjudicate the fight; he just lays out both indictments and lets them sit. The man who spent May 2023 explaining why the GOP couldn't escape Trump is now explaining why the Democrats can't escape themselves. The symmetry is the point: both parties are hostage situations now.

## 7. The victory-lap errors, stated plainly

A victory lap is the worst place for a factual error, because the errors land on the paragraphs where the author is claiming authority. There is one here, and it is worth stating plainly without cruelty:

> "Paul Ryan has exited; McConnell is no longer speaker."

Mitch McConnell was the Senate Republican *leader*, never the Speaker of the House — the speakership belongs to the House, and McConnell never held it. He announced in February 2024 that he would step down as leader; John Thune was elected to succeed him on November 13, 2024 — the day *after* this post went up. So the sentence is wrong on the title and premature by a day on the transition. Small, but it's in the paragraph where he's surveying the conquered landscape, which is where errors sting most.

The pattern is the documented one, and it is worth naming because it is the article's thesis about the man: **the analysis is reliable; the autobiography of the analysis drifts.** The mechanism of the DeSantis call was airtight; the memory of when he called the general was inflated by six months and a degree of certainty the contemporary text doesn't support. The lawfare read was genuinely earned; the title under which it's filed was never the office. Good-faith misremembering that flatters the narrator, never fabrication. His own ledger says stated certainty runs ≈ 0.25 actual — and here, in the victory lap, is the ledger proving itself. The correct response is not to discount the analysis (the primary call remains one of the best contrarian calls of the cycle) but to timestamp every memory against the corpus. Trust the corpus over him on years. He said so himself.

## 8. The closer and the unfulfilled promise

The post ends in two moves. First, the conversion of the Substack into a watchtower: "This may become an outlet for tracking what happens next. It will be interesting, if nothing else, to watch what unfolds when we hand the reins to someone who is, by any reasonable standard, unqualified — and let him off the leash." Second, the last line of the corpus:

> "Brace yourself for… something."

The ellipsis is doing the work the whole hell-world frame can no longer do. "Strap in" (June 2023) was a thrill ride; "Brace yourself for… something" is a man who has run out of apocalypse jokes because the apocalypse showed up and it turned out to be boring in the worst way — not the cinematic kind, the administrative kind, the kind where an unqualified man gets handed the leash and the mandate and the judiciary and there is nothing left to file a dispatch about because the dispatch already wrote itself.

And then the watchtower never filed another dispatch. The thirteen-month silence before November 2024 became permanent — no tracking of what happened next, no outlet, no follow-up. The deep reading offers three hypotheses for the silence, in descending order of evidence: the thesis was spent (the founding insight confirmed within months, Poll Watch euthanized after two installments); he only writes on conviction (the silence isn't absence — "with every development, my confidence grew" means he was watching the whole time); and the joke died (the hell-world frame works as comedy when the doom is prospective; after real corpses in October 2023 and a real victory in November 2024, the bit can't carry the weight). The corpus is ten posts, May 2023 to November 2024, and its shape is itself a finding: a man who writes when he has a thesis and goes quiet when he doesn't — and whose final thesis was that the thing he predicted had happened, and there was nothing left to say except "…something."

**The verdict on the claim:** Dan Frank called the 2024 GOP nomination in May 2023 with total confidence, on mechanism, against the media consensus, and he was completely right. He tracked the general election in real time with explicit hedges, a Biden-favoring framework relayed straight, and a confidence that grew as the evidence arrived — directionally right, honestly earned. The November 2024 post remembers it as a single two-years-out call. It wasn't. It was better than that and messier than that: the best primary call of the cycle, and a general-election read that did the work in public, one development at a time. The retrofit flatters the teller; the archive flatters him more, because the archive shows the work.
