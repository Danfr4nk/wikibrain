# Alex Frank

Alex Frank is Dan's cousin on the paternal Frank line, a Brooklyn-based music
and culture journalist, and the author of the one piece of family writing Dan
has kept for a decade and still retells: a short essay about Morley Frank,
Dan's grandfather, and the boxes of frowning cookies his widow ordered for his
funeral ([`dat:0199`](../../kb/data/0199-alex-frank-cousin-and-frownie-cookie.md)).
In Dan's words to [[wiki/people/annie-ulmer|Annie]] on 20 November 2017, he is
*"my cousin Alex that writes for Vogue"*
(`raw/imessage/messages-part1-2011-2019.csv`, 2017-11-20 22:14:59 UTC).

The relationship the record shows is thin, warm, and almost entirely
one-directional in what survives: a mutual Instagram follow since 2012, three
public replies from Dan on Twitter, one planned meet-up in Uniontown, one
reported conversation in New York, a place on Dan's Instagram Close Friends list,
and a story Alex wrote that reached Dan through his father and became part of
how Dan explains where his sense of humour comes from. No message thread
between the two of them exists in any held archive. What exists is the
residue of a cousin Dan was proud of, followed closely, and occasionally
reached.

## The story he wrote

The essay is the reason this page exists. It is reproduced verbatim from the
copy Dan uploaded to ChatGPT on 22 July 2025 (`raw/chatgpt/dfrank88-2025-08-05/dfrank88 5 AUG 2025/chat.html`,
file `Pap pap frown cookies.pdf`), which matches the prior page's quotation
word for word ([`dat:0199`](../../kb/data/0199-alex-frank-cousin-and-frownie-cookie.md)):

> I always tell this story about my Uncle Morley. He was a real pisser, you know? You might have an Uncle Morley in your family, but this guy made the mold. His humor was a mix of self-deprecation, sexual harassment, and insult, but his endearing, crooked half-smile (my Grandpa had the exact same grin) kept the smart, well-humored ones from being too hurt by his jokes. I was too young to quite get it, but I did get a sense of no-bullshit from him that children always respond to. Anyway, there's this diner in Pittsburgh, Eat'n Park that's famous for making frosted smily face cookies. They've been doing it for decades, and they're one of those Pittsburgh symbols that unite us (The Original, Primanti's sandwiches, Mineo's: they're all food, of course, we're fat, okay?). Anyway, everyday Uncle Morley stopped at the Eat'n Park by his house for a coffee, stared at the shelves and shelves of Eat'n Park cookies mocking him with their smiles and asked the cashier one thing: "I'm having a shit day, can't you be fair and make me a frownie face cookie?" They laughed off his self-deprecating routine like anyone would. But he asked everyday. And they never gave it to him.
>
> When Uncle Morley died, his wife Sis had boxes of frownie face cookies made up from the local Eat'n Park for his funeral party. When we all walked in and saw the inverted symbol strewn amongst the more generic catered items, it expressed a certain kind of familial understanding that comes from generations of genetic personality traits, like a fucked up sense of humor and melancholy lense on life. The more earnest funeral goers didn't get the joke, I suspect. But the smart, well-humored ones did. And they're the only ones you want at the party anyway.

What the essay records is a joke that took years to land. Morley asked the
same cashier the same question every day; the answer was always no; the
punchline arrived after he was dead, delivered by his wife, to a room that
split along exactly the line Alex draws — the ones who got it and the ones who
did not. The funeral was in December 1998: Morley Jay Frank died in Hopwood on
13 December 1998 and is buried there
([`dat:1115`](../../kb/data/1115-morley-frank-residence-record-and-personal-contact.md);
[[wiki/people/morley-frank]]). Dan was nine or ten.

Two details in the essay do quiet work. *"My Grandpa had the exact same
grin"* places Alex's grandfather beside Morley as a sibling, which is the
anchor for the kinship section below. And the essay's last sentence is a
sorting rule — the funeral as a test that separates the people worth having
from the rest — which is recognisably the same rule Dan applies to humour in
his own messages (§What Dan does with it).

## How the story reached Dan

The prior version of this page listed as a gap *"whether Alex's funeral essay
was published somewhere findable or shared privately within the family."* The
ChatGPT upload answers most of it, because Dan uploaded not a transcription but
a clipped email.

The file Dan attached on 22 July 2025 is an Evernote web clip. Its header reads
*"SAVE!!!!"* above a Gmail link, and the clipped message is titled
**"Fwd: Alex Frank Blog Piece"**
(`raw/chatgpt/dfrank88-2025-08-05/dfrank88 5 AUG 2025/chat.html`, tether quote
created 2025-07-23 02:45:16 UTC). Below the essay the clip preserves two
forwarding layers:

1. The essay arrives inside an email carrying the signature block of a
   Pittsburgh attorney surnamed Frank. The record does not show his relation
   to Alex or to Dan, and his contact details are not reproduced here.
2. That message was forwarded to Dan by **[[wiki/people/rick-frank|Rick Frank]]**,
   Dan's father — the clip's footer reads *"rick frank … Jun 2, 2016, 1:21 PM
   / to me."*

So: Alex wrote it as a blog piece; it travelled through the family by email; it
reached Dan from his father on **2 June 2016**; Dan clipped it to Evernote with
four exclamation marks; and nine years later he still had the file. Where the
blog piece was originally published is not in the record — the clip carries no
URL for the post itself, only for the Gmail message. That narrower gap stays
open.

The chain also accounts for a discrepancy the family tree already caught. The
2025-09-03 frownie line was once read as a reference to a different
grandfather's death around that date; Dan corrected it, and
[[wiki/self/lineage/family-tree]] carries the REVISED note. The mechanism of the
error is now visible: an auto-extracted calendar dated the retelling, not the
funeral.

## What Dan does with it

The story is not something Dan cites. It is something he uses — to explain
himself, and to test whether the person he is talking to gets the joke. Three
dated uses survive.

**28 April 2018 — "a lot of morley in me."** Talking through a work conflict
with a correspondent whose handle is unresolved, Dan is told to let it go. He
answers: *"you're right but i've got a lot of morley in me"*, then *"if there's
one lesson morley taught me it was / don't be an asshole, be a sonofabitch"*,
then *"also 'you only break balls with the people you care about'"*
(`raw/imessage/messages-part1-2011-2019.csv`, 2018-04-28 23:13:21–23:15:17 UTC;
first two lines recorded in
[`dat:1115`](../../kb/data/1115-morley-frank-residence-record-and-personal-contact.md)).
The third line was not previously on any page. It is the essay's thesis in
Morley's own reported words: the insult as a form of regard, reserved for the
people it would not actually hurt.

**22 July 2025 — the family, explained to a model.** Dan opens a ChatGPT
conversation titled *"Family Mythos Unleashed"* with *"GPT we're gonna hit a
TOTALLY new area of interest here. Let me share something with you about my
family:"* and attaches the clip. The model reads "Uncle Morley" literally.
Dan's correction comes about half a minute after its reply: *"well morley isn't
uncle. morley is grandfather. the father of.....MY FATHER."* He then walks the
model through the family business — links to Herald-Standard coverage of
Frank's Auto Supermarket and a 2006 store advert — and states the lineage in
one line: *"My great grandfather started that store in the early 20th century.
my grandfather was a real character and a rare breed."*
(`raw/chatgpt/dfrank88-2025-08-05/dfrank88 5 AUG 2025/chat.html`,
2025-07-23 02:45:04–02:51:49 UTC; no message archive holds this). The conversation turns to Rick from there
and is outside this page's subject.

**3 September 2025 — the story as a test.** In a late-evening exchange with
[[wiki/people/kristin|Kristin]], Dan writes *"okay now THIS is a byproduct of
growing up in a jewish family"*, *"no joke"*, then *"eat n' park delivered
frownie cookies to my granfather's funeral i swear to god lmao"*. She answers
*"Lmfao"* and *"Best story I ever heard I'm so sorry RIP"*. He supplies the
mechanism — *"they did it because he went there every day and used the same
line"* — and the date: *"sheeeeet eppppppp also it was 1998 lol / different
world / fuck im old."* Minutes later she scores it back to him: *"Mmm idk the
frownie cookie is a close one ☝🏼 / Frownie. Lol"*
(`raw/imessage/messages-part2-2019-2026.csv`, 2025-09-03 23:28:23–23:36:27 UTC).
Immediately before the story Dan had told her *"i never joke around with the
people i don't like or am ambivelant about"* — the same rule as Morley's
*"you only break balls with the people you care about,"* seven years on.

That is the pattern across all three: the story is deployed at the moment Dan
is explaining that his abrasiveness is inherited and affectionate, and it is
received as intended. Kristin passes the essay's own test.

## The kinship, reckoned

The prior page stated two things that cannot both follow from its own chain:
that *"Dan's grandfather and Alex's grandfather were brothers"* and that this
makes Alex Dan's *"first cousin once removed"*, with Morley as *"the shared
great-uncle."*

What the record fixes:

- **Morley is Dan's paternal grandfather.** The family tree places him there
  ([[wiki/self/lineage/family-tree]]: David J. Frank + Sadie Harris → Morley →
  Rick → Dan); Dan says so twice in his own words (*"my granfather's
  funeral"*, 2025-09-03; *"morley is grandfather. the father of.....MY
  FATHER"*, 2025-07-22); and the file he uploaded is named *"Pap pap frown
  cookies"*.
- **Morley is Alex's "Uncle Morley,"** and Alex's grandfather *"had the exact
  same grin"* — consistent with Alex's grandfather being Morley's brother.

If Dan's grandfather (Morley) and Alex's grandfather were brothers, then Rick
and Alex's parent are first cousins, and **Dan and Alex are second cousins** —
same generation, shared great-grandparents (David J. Frank and Sadie Harris, if
the brother is a full sibling). Morley is Dan's grandfather and Alex's
great-uncle; he is not a great-uncle *shared* by both. "First cousin once
removed" would require Alex to be a first cousin of Rick, i.e. a generation
up, which nothing in the record says.

Two caveats keep this from being more than a reckoning. First, "Uncle" in a
family essay can be affectionate rather than exact; the record holds no
sibling list for Morley in any held extract of the ancestry tree, so the
brother is inferred from the essay, not documented. Second, the ancestry
extract the prior page cited (`raw/self/ancestry/extracted/Daniel Frank family
tree.txt`) is not held, so its Find a Grave memorial number (#201542137) is
carried as the prior wiki's
([`dat:0199`](../../kb/data/0199-alex-frank-cousin-and-frownie-cookie.md)).
The infobox now reads "second cousin on the page's own stated chain."

## Appendix — why "extract-only" is marked

[`CORPUS_POLICY.md`](../../CORPUS_POLICY.md) makes the 192,140-row Messages
export the only tier that may support a message-derived claim, and says a line
that appears only in a fragment is *"a bug to investigate, not a fact to
keep."* The three lines marked extract-only come from
`raw/takeout/takeout-20260103T040931Z-3-001/Takeout/My Activity/Gemini
Apps/all_imessages_part_*.txt`, a multi-contact extract uploaded to Gemini in
January 2026, eight months before the authoritative export.

They are absent from the corpus for a structural reason that can be measured.
The corpus holds 39 rows in total on Rick's handle and 170 on Suz's in the
2011–2019 part file, and Annie's AOL iMessage address first appears in April 2016 — after the
February 2016 tuxedo exchange (`raw/imessage/messages-part1-2011-2019.csv`,
counted 2026-09-25). The adjacent Annie-handle lines on 2017-11-20 are present
in both files with the same text and a five-hour offset (the extract is local,
the corpus UTC), which is the expected signature of two exports of one
database, not of a fabricated file. That makes the three lines plausible, not
verified. They are logged here as an investigation item under the policy, not
as facts the page rests on; nothing in the kinship, provenance or corrections
sections depends on them.
