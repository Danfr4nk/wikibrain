## Personal bearing

The concern is genuinely shared — self-deceiving cognition is a standing object of Dan's documented practice — and the treatments are opposites, which is what makes this a usable contrast case rather than a dismissal. On the calibrated-confidence page, Dan's habit is to put a number on a belief, keep the number attached to its evidence, and recompute when the corpus changes (the page's own headline figure was recomputed downward by exactly that discipline). Wiest's lists ask the reader to do the same work by recognition: spot the category in yourself, feel the hit, move on. No item carries a frequency, a severity, or a way to be wrong. The CBT catalogue underneath Essay 91 is real clinical material; what the book adds is only the list form, and the list form is the part that cannot grade anything.

## Evidence

Phase 1 adjudicated the two lists separately: Essay 91's fifteen distortions trace to McKay, Davis & Fanning's CBT catalogue and are the book's most technically grounded material (high confidence the source and categories are real; the essay adds no evidence that spotting a category corrects it). Essay 16's bias list is mixed (medium confidence overall): four real named effects, two of Wiest's own constructions presented as list-mates, and clustering illusion folded into confirmation.

## Limits

A contrast case, not a source: nothing from either list should be cited on the wiki as a taxonomy — the wiki's own calibration pages and the CBT literature itself are the citable homes. And the contrast cuts only as far as it is documented: Dan's graded practice is itself partly testimony-grade on its own page, with recomputations recorded where its numbers failed to reproduce. The difference this node records is in the form of the treatment — graded versus ungraded — not a claim that one side is error-free.
