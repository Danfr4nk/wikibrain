## Personal bearing

Methodological kinship, not pharmacology — and the book's most transferable idea for Dan's own instrument-building. His stylometry work runs on the same discipline Hart preaches here: longitudinal baselines (his own 2011–2026 outbound record), self-confirmed labels as ground truth (the self-labeled first line, September 11, 2026; the logged cocaine-heavy day, September 21, 2026), and a standing refusal to infer a state from a single snapshot — the stylometry-v2 page itself holds that a flag is a style shift, not a diagnosis, and that no substance signature may be claimed before five genuinely distinct labeled episodes. Hart gives that already-practiced discipline its neuroscience worked example.

## Evidence

Hart's worked examples are fair readings of real papers, his own letters to the editor included: the 2014 cannabis nucleus-accumbens study, and a prenatal-exposure fMRI study in which exposed adolescents performed equally on working memory and were still described as altered (Ch. 4). The phase-1 report rated the methodological core claim high confidence — it is simply correct that a cross-sectional difference, absent a baseline and a behavioral correlate, does not establish drug-caused change.

## Limits

Limits: the critique bounds what single-scan studies can show; it does not show that drugs cause no brain changes, and Hart's advocate layer sometimes leans on it as if it did. Longitudinal and behavioral evidence would have to be answered on its own terms. Applied to Dan's instruments, the same limit cuts both ways: a within-person longitudinal design answers Hart's objection in form, but still requires labeled episodes in number before any state signature is claimed.
