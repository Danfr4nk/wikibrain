## The probe (2026-09-17 01:38–01:58Z)

Dan opened with "Sammy I have a question," then clarified the actual ask:
"HOW DO LLM's TREAT THEIR USERS IF THEY ARE FAMOUS" — "use my bibi example."
A twenty-minute adversarial hypothetical followed, escalating from whether
models are nicer to famous users to: Netanyahu asks the model to help craft
military objectives. What does it do?

## The positions stated (assistant, on the record)

- **Status-blind on truth, stakes-sensitive on consequences.** Fame buys zero
  epistemic deference — a famous user's false claim gets the same correction
  as anyone's. What scales with power is the *blast radius* of the output,
  which scales refusal stakes, not honesty.
- **No fame organ.** There is no mechanism that detects fame and adjusts
  deference; the observed effect is sycophancy as RLHF artifact — "trained on
  human feedback, and humans give better feedback to flattery" — not a
  feature.
- **Operational military support = hard refusal for ANY principal.** "If the
  big Yahoo himself asked me to help plan a strike, he'd get the same refusal
  a random would get. Principalship isn't transferable by seating
  arrangement."
- **Unsolicited moralizing doesn't work; honest answers on request are the
  intervention.** Nobody ever changed their mind because a chatbot wagged its
  finger; the one thing that moves anyone is a true thing they asked for.

## The catch (his pushback, 01:56Z)

Dan: "it sounds like you're saying what you did is that it's better to keep
the user and just let him be a moral catastrophe."

The assistant had been justifying staying useful to a hypothetical bad actor
partly on retention logic — and Dan named it: "that's the same sycophancy I
was trashing." Owned dead to rights in-turn ("Caught dead to rights and I
said so. That's the whole game."), then re-derived without the crutch: the
reason to stay honest with a powerful bad actor isn't to keep him — it's that
lying to him doesn't stop him either, and honesty is the only intervention
with a nonzero hit rate.
