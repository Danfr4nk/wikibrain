Quartz (a Caltech neuroscientist) and Asp (a political scientist turned neuromarketing practitioner) argue that consumption is driven neither by rational utility, nor by advertising-manufactured false needs, nor by Veblen-style zero-sum status warfare, but by two evolved instincts: a status instinct - a drive to be valued as a social partner, tracked by a brain sociometer - and a rebel instinct, a refusal of subordination inherited from coalition politics and hunter-gatherer reverse dominance hierarchies. Rebel cool in the 1950s multiplied the routes to status; DotCool in the 1990s re-pointed the same machinery at the knowledge economy's values (creativity, unconventionality, learning). Because status dimensions can multiply, the book claims cool dissolves the Status Dilemma, and that the same signaling machinery can be aimed at ethical and green consumption.

Phase-1 reliability finding: a split book. What survives audit is the behavioral and sociological layer - the visible-spending econometrics (Charles, Hurst & Roussanov), the Tsimane expenditure pattern, Berger & Heath's divergence experiments, Sexton & Sexton's Prius premium on real registration data, the local-status literature, and the dopamine-as-prediction-error core of Chapter 2. What does not survive: the flagship 2004 cool-circuit scan (unpublished, television-origin, reverse inference), the Lacoste alligator effect (failed double-blind replication, Berger, PLOS ONE 2017), and decorative evolutionary garnish (facial width-to-height ratio, ovulatory preference shifts, birth-order rebels) that has collapsed under later large-sample work. The status-as-currency and divergence themes are conceptual arguments carried by the surviving layer, not by any brain scan.
