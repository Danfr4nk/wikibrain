Carl L. Hart's argument, from twenty-five years of NIDA-funded laboratory work giving crack, cocaine, methamphetamine, cannabis and other drugs to human volunteers at Columbia, that the predominant effects of the drugs he studies are positive, only a minority of users ever meet addiction criteria, catastrophic outcomes are produced mainly by prohibition itself (contaminated supply, ignorance, arrest, stigma) and by poverty and psychiatric distress, and autonomous adults therefore have a right to buy, sell and use drugs under legal regulation. He stakes the book on a disclosure: he is himself a regular recreational heroin user, five years in at writing, alongside cocaine, MDMA and amphetamines.

Phase-1 reliability finding, by layer: Hart's own laboratory findings are sound within their limits (acute doses, screened experienced volunteers, small samples); his literature synthesis is fair except at two endpoints — the Portugal figures are presented at their most favorable reading and the U.S. comparison figure does not survive arithmetic, and the Prologue's moderate-alcohol benefit claim rests on the older J-curve literature that abstainer-bias corrections, Mendelian randomization and the 2018 Global Burden of Disease analysis had already badly weakened by publication. The advocacy layer borrows the laboratory layer's authority: the heroin-assisted-treatment trials he cites studied refractory, treatment-resistant patients inside an intensive medical wrap, not recreational use by healthy professionals, and his self-experimentation is an n of one highly atypical man. Trust the critic of brain-imaging overreach, media drug panics and sentencing disparity at full value; read the advocate-memoirist as a witness for the defense who has genuinely been in the room.
